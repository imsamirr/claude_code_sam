My finger was half an inch above the little glass signature pad on the finance desk.

The plastic stylus was in my hand.

On the screen, in bold digital letters, was the total: $2,600.00.

Right below it: 24 monthly payments of $128.45.

My purse was sitting on my lap in the consultation room at the denture clinic on Hendersonville Road in Asheville.

The air smelled like mint floor wax and rubbing alcohol.

The credit rep, a young woman with neat French-tip nails, turned the pad toward me.

"Just sign on the line, Mrs. Gable," she said with a practiced smile. "We will take your impressions today and have your new upper plate ready in two weeks."

My hand was shaking.

Not from arthritis.

Because $2,600 was almost my entire emergency savings from thirty years of managing the cafeteria at Reynolds High.

Right then, my phone buzzed against the laminate counter.

A text from my 19-year-old grandson, Dylan.

Dylan works down on Tunnel Road at an auto-detailing and precision engine shop.

He usually texts about football or asks if I baked banana bread.

Not today.

His text was six words:

"Grandma, put that pen down now."

I froze.

My finger hovered over the glass.

I looked up at the credit girl.

"Give me two minutes," I muttered. "My phone is ringing."

I walked out into the brisk October wind in the parking lot and hit his number.

That thirty-second phone call saved me $2,500.

And it showed me the truth about what was actually happening to my teeth.

For the past year, I thought my upper plate was worn out.

I had worn it for four years.

It smelled sour and stale, like a damp sponge that never dried out.

Yellow stains had settled into the crevices around the gumline, and no matter what I did, they kept darkening.

By midday, my mouth tasted like old copper.

My gums burned.

The entire roof of my mouth was red, raw, and stinging.

I could not eat hot soup or crusty bread without wincing.

I kept Wintergreen mints in every cardigan pocket because I was terrified people at church or the grocery store could smell my plate.

So I went to the denture clinic.

The sales rep looked at my plate in a paper cup, shook his head, and sighed.

"Acrylic is porous, Mrs. Gable," he said. "After four years, the plastic breaks down and absorbs mouth fluids. Once that white film and smell settle into the material, you cannot clean it out. You need new teeth. That will be $2,600."

I believed him.

I thought I had ruined them.

Because heaven knows, I had tried everything to clean them.

Here is the ledger of what I had already thrown at this problem:

$18 a box every month for four years.

That is over $860 right there.

Those bubbles just pop on the slick surface. They never reach inside the material.

$12 a tube for paste, and two stiff-bristle brushes at $9 each.

I scrubbed at the bathroom sink until my wrists ached.

What nobody told me is that stiff nylon bristles carve microscopic scratches across soft acrylic.

Every scrub gouged tiny valleys into the plate, giving bacteria and yeast more places to burrow.

Third was white vinegar and baking soda soaks.

$4 a gallon from the Ingles grocery store.

A woman on Facebook swore vinegar dissolves film.

All it did was make my mouth taste like salad dressing until noon, while the yellow stains and white film stayed put.

Fourth was the $30 "jewelry and denture pod" from Amazon.

I bought it eight months ago.

A cheap plastic tub that buzzed like an angry hornet on my counter.

It vibrated weakly for three weeks, then the motor burned out completely.

The water never got warm, and my plate stayed slimy.

I thought I had no choices left.

That was why I was sitting at that finance desk.

When Dylan pulled into the clinic parking lot on his lunch break, I walked over to his truck.

I showed him my plate in the plastic travel cup.

"The man inside says the plastic is worn out," I said. "He says that is why it has that white film and smells sour. He says there is no way to clean it."
