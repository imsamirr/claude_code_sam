## PRIMARY TEXT

Three times in one year, my dentist sent me home with a yeast rinse.
Three times, it worked.
And three times, the burn came back in about 21 days.

I counted. I was a bank teller for 31 years. I count everything.
What nobody counted was where the yeast was coming back from.
It took a 29-year-old hygienist and a little bottle of purple dye to show me.
It was in my mouth every single night.
I put it there myself.

Let me back up.

I'm Bev. I'm 63. I live in Mesa, Arizona.
I counted cash at the same branch on Main Street for 31 years.
I've worn an upper denture for nine of them.

The first dentist told me, "Wear it as much as you can, so you get used to it."
So I did. Day and night. For nine years.
Nobody ever told me to stop.

I cleaned it every morning.
A Polident tablet in a cup of warm water while I showered.
A quick brush. Back in.
I thought I was the cleanest denture wearer in Maricopa County.

Then, a little over a year ago, the roof of my mouth started to burn.
Not a sore spot. A burn.
Like I'd taken a big bite of pizza straight out of the oven, every day, all day.
My coffee tasted like pennies.
Everything tasted a little off, like the food had been sitting out.

I looked in the bathroom mirror with my phone flashlight.
The roof of my mouth was red.
Not all of it.
Just a patch the exact shape of my plate.

Here's what I lost that year, one piece at a time.

Coffee on the patio with my husband, Ron.
Every morning at sunrise, before it gets hot, for thirty-some years.
Then the coffee tasted like a mouthful of change, and I stopped going out.

Lunch with the girls from the branch, first Wednesday of every month.
I quit going because I couldn't stand what everything tasted like.
And I was scared of my own breath.

And Friday pizza night.
Our grandkids, Mateo and Lily, come over every Friday.
We'd go to the pizza place on Southern Avenue and they'd fight over the last pepperoni slice.
Hot pizza on that burning palate felt like fire.
So I started ordering a salad.
Then I started staying home.
Lily asked her mom, "Is Grandma Bev mad at pizza?"

PRESCRIPTION ONE. January.
The dentist took one look. "That's denture stomatitis. It's a yeast infection under your plate."
He wrote me an antifungal rinse. Swish, four times a day, two weeks.
By day five, the burn was fading.
By day fourteen, it was gone.
I almost cried in the kitchen I was so happy.
Twenty-two days later, it was back.

PRESCRIPTION TWO. May.
Same rinse. Same two weeks. Same relief.
This time I decided the Polident wasn't strong enough.
I bought a $30 ultrasonic cleaner from Amazon.
White plastic. One button. No light of any kind.
I ran it every morning with a Polident tablet in it.
It buzzed. The water fizzed. I felt like I was doing something.
By week three, the buzz was weaker.
By week four, it didn't turn on at all.
It's in my junk drawer with the cord wrapped around it.
Nineteen days after the rinse ran out, the burn came back.

PRESCRIPTION THREE. August.
Lily's sixth birthday. August 9th. 114 degrees.
She wanted pizza, of course.
I told myself I'd eat one slice for her.
I took one bite.
Hot cheese on the raw roof of my mouth.
I had to spit it into a napkin at my granddaughter's birthday party.
I went into the restaurant bathroom at 6:40 p.m., locked the door, and cried.
Ron knocked. "Bev? Honey?"
I said I was fine.
I wasn't fine. I was 63 years old, hiding in a bathroom from a pizza.

Monday, I went back for the third rinse.
The dentist wasn't wrong. The rinse worked. Every time.
It just never stayed.

This time, there was a new hygienist. Tasha. Twenty-nine. Purple glasses.
She asked me two questions nobody had asked in a year.
"Bev, do you sleep in your upper?"
Every night. For nine years.
"What do you clean it with?"
I told her. Polident. And the $30 machine, until it died.

She held out her hand. "Can I see it?"
She took my plate and painted the inside, the part that sits against the roof of my mouth, with a little brush.
Purple dye. They call it disclosing solution. It sticks to buildup.
Then she rinsed it.
Most of the plate came out clean pink.
But the inside was covered in purple patches.
Blotchy. Spread out.
The same shape as the red on my palate.

"That's where it lives," she said.
"You're treating your mouth, not your denture."

I just stared at her.

She explained it like this.
The plate looks smooth. Under a microscope, the acrylic is full of tiny pores and scratches.
Candida, the yeast, gets down into them and holds on.
The rinse clears your palate. Then every morning you put the plate back in.
And the yeast moves right back onto clean, healed tissue.
"It reseeds you every single morning," she said.
"And then you sleep in it. Warm, wet, dark, pressed against your palate for eight hours. That's a greenhouse."

She said studies find this in up to about two-thirds of denture wearers.
And the two big risk factors are sleeping in your dentures and how they're cleaned.
I had both. For nine years.

Then she found the thing that made it click.
"You live in Mesa. Do you have a pool?"
Of course I have a pool.
"When it turns green, what do you do?"
Shock it.
"And if you never clean the filter?"
I didn't even have to answer.
Green again in three weeks.
Three weeks.
I'd been shocking the pool and putting the dirty filter right back in. Three times.

So what about the Polident?
Tasha said a tablet mostly works on the surface. It fizzes over the top while the yeast sits down in the pores.
And mine only soaked for the length of a shower. The other 23 hours, the plate was in my mouth.

And the $30 machine?
"Ultrasonic is the right idea," she said. "It shakes things loose from the pores."
"But shaking it loose isn't killing it. It's still alive, floating in the water, and you put your plate right back in that water."
"Ultrasonic does one job. UV-C light does the other."

Before I left, she gave me a test. You can do it tonight.
Take your plate out before bed.
Stand at the mirror with your phone flashlight and look at the roof of your mouth.
If there's a red patch the exact shape of your plate, show your dentist.
Mine had been there for a year.

I went home and did what I did for 31 years at the bank.
I researched it like a loan file.

That's how I found the test.
A 90-day, side-by-side lab test of seven leading ultrasonic cleaners.
Six of the seven left live bacteria behind.
Six out of seven.
And my $30 one didn't even have a light.

Shaking loose and killing are two different jobs.
So I wrote my list on the back of a deposit slip.
1. Ultrasonic strong enough to reach down into the pores.
2. Medical-grade UV-C light, for whatever the sound shakes loose.
3. Tap water. I was done with the blue cup.
4. A real basin, not thin plastic that quits in a month.
5. A guarantee. The rinse never came with one. Neither did the $30 machine.

Only one cleaner in that test came out on top.
DentalPULSE.

42,000 Hz of ultrasonic cleaning to get into the pores.
Medical-grade UV-C light in the same cycle.
One button. One 5-minute cycle. Tap water only. No tablets, no subscription.
A stainless steel basin.
It eliminates 99.9% of bacteria in one 5-minute cycle.
Dentist-recommended, and rated 4.8 out of 5.
$99.95.

I'll be honest. I almost didn't.
I'd already been burned for thirty dollars.
Ron said, "Bev. You counted money for 31 years. Count this one."
So I did.
Three rinse copays. Two extra office visits. The $30 machine in the drawer. A year of Polident.
Well over $300, and I still had the burn.
$99.95, once. And 120 days to send it back if I was wrong.

DAY 1.
I was on rinse number three, day nine.
I'd been sleeping without my plate for a week by then. First time in nine years.
Ron said I sounded like a toothless pirate. I threw a pillow at him.
That morning, I took the plate out of its new case.
Tap water to the line. Plate in. One button.
It hummed for five minutes.
When I lifted the plate out, the water was cloudy.
Little white specks drifting at the bottom of the steel.
From a plate I'd soaked in Polident the morning before.
That was in my mouth, I thought.
Every morning. For a year.

WEEK 1.
Honest week.
Nothing felt different. I was still on the rinse, so of course it felt better.
It always felt better on the rinse.
That wasn't the test.
The test was three weeks after the rinse ran out.

DAY 6.
The rinse ran out.
The plate slept in its case. Every morning, five minutes in the Pod while the coffee brewed.

DAY 27.
Three weeks since the last swish. The day it always came back.
I stood at the mirror with my phone flashlight like I'd done a hundred times.
Pink.
I checked again that night. Pink.

DAY 34.
Ron carried two cups out to the patio at sunrise.
I followed him.
The coffee tasted like coffee.
I sat there until the sun came over the mountains and it was too hot to stay.

WEEK 7.
First Wednesday in October.
I walked into lunch with the girls from the branch.
Dolores yelled, "Look who's back from the dead!"
I had the enchiladas. All of them.

WEEK 10.
Checkup. Tasha again.
She painted the plate with the purple dye.
Rinsed it.
Almost nothing.
She held it up to the light and grinned at me.
The dentist looked at my palate. "Whatever you're doing, keep doing it."

I need to be straight with you here.
I'm not a doctor. I can't tell you the Pod cured anything.
The rinse cleared my mouth. It cleared it three times before, too.
Taking the plate out at night gave my palate a rest.
What changed this time was the plate.
For the first time, something cleaned the plate instead of just my mouth.
And it's been ten weeks since the last rinse. That's three of my old 21-day clocks, and then some.

LAST FRIDAY.
It finally dropped under 90.
Mateo and Lily came over and we ate on the patio.
Pepperoni, extra cheese, straight out of the box, still hot.
I had three slices.
Lily watched me take the third one and said, "Grandma's not mad at pizza anymore."
No, baby. Grandma's not mad at pizza anymore.

Those three amber bottles sat on my bathroom windowsill all year.
I kept them there so I'd remember to count.
I put them in the recycling Sunday.
I don't need to count anymore.
