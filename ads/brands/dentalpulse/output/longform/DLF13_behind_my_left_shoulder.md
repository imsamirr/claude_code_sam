## PRIMARY TEXT

I was standing near the dessert table at my grandson's wedding reception in Albuquerque when I heard two voices right behind my left shoulder.

"Do not get too close when she talks," one whispered.

"The smell coming off her mouth is like an old wet dish sponge."

I froze with a cup of coffee halfway to my lips.

The room had eighty people in it.

My twenty-four-year-old grandson Tyler was dancing with his bride across the wooden floor.

I did not turn around.

My face went hot, that stinging prickle of heat that crawls up your neck when you realize you are the person everyone is pitying.

Reflected in the patio glass, I saw who was talking: Brenda, my daughter-in-law's older sister, and her adult daughter Ashley.

Brenda held a napkin near her nose.

Ashley whispered back, "I know. It was bad in the receiving line. Is it her teeth?"

Brenda nodded. "She has false teeth. That paste rots under the plastic and she does not even realize."

I set my cup down, my hand shaking so hard the saucer rattled.

I did not say goodbye to Tyler or my son.

I slipped out into the parking lot and sat in my Buick for forty minutes in the dark.

I am sixty-nine years old.

For thirty-two years, I played organ at St. Paul's on Constitution Avenue, and I have taught piano since 1989.

Eight years ago, after severe gum disease, my dentist fitted me with a full upper acrylic denture plate.

Nobody ever told me I had bad breath.

My husband passed six years ago, so there was nobody at home to give me an honest warning.

My church friends were too polite, and my own family had apparently been taking a half-step backward every time I spoke.

I sat in that dark car and rubbed the roof of my mouth with my tongue. It felt hot and raw, like it had felt for six months. I had told myself it was just spicy chile or shrinking gums.

When I got home, I went straight to the mirror and pulled my upper plate down.

The suction broke with a wet pop.

The smell that hit my nose made my stomach twist: a sour, damp, sewer-like smell, like a flooded basement that never dried out.

Along the deep groove where the plastic hugs the gumline was a thick, yellowish gray sludge.

It was gummy, like melted rubber mixed with grease.

Every morning, I squeezed three ribbons of pink Fixodent paste into that groove to lock the plate in place.

Every night, I dropped it into a mug of warm water with a blue Polident tablet, watched it fizz, and scrubbed it with a denture brush.

I thought the blue bubbles were cleaning it.

They were not.

I scraped my thumb along that groove. A curl of greasy sludge came off under my nail. I smelled it and gagged: rotten yeast and trapped bacteria.

In the mirror, the roof of my mouth was bright crimson, raw, and dotted with tiny red spots. Dentists call that denture stomatitis.

It happens when Candida yeast and bacteria burrow into the microscopic pores of the acrylic and multiply in the dark space between the plastic and your palate.

Every morning, that sticky paste was sealing that bacteria and yeast against my gums for fourteen hours.

And fizzy tablets are useless against it.

A tablet is just surface detergent and bleach.

It makes bubbles, but cannot dissolve sticky paste and cannot reach into microscopic pores where bacterial biofilm anchors itself.

I tried to fix it myself.

First, I bought two stiff-bristled denture brushes and heavy scrubbing paste ($14 at the drugstore).

I scrubbed until my knuckles hurt.

All that did was scratch the soft acrylic, creating new microscopic trenches where more bacteria and yellow stains could hide.

Next, I doubled up on tablets: Polident every morning, Efferdent every night ($18 a month, over $200 a year forever).

My mouth burned even worse.

Back in 2008, the FDA issued a public health notice warning about persulfate in denture cleansers because it causes allergic reactions, tissue burns, and mouth inflammation.

I was burning the roof of my own mouth.

Then a neighbor told me to soak it in diluted bleach and vinegar.

That damaged the soft liner, tasted foul, and the sour smell returned in two hours.

Desperate, I bought a $30 ultrasonic pod off Amazon.

It buzzed on the counter, had zero UV light, and died after three weeks.

In an independent 90-day lab test of seven leading ultrasonic cleaners, six out of seven machines left live bacteria on dental appliances overnight.

That $30 Amazon pod was one of the failures.

I felt completely cornered.

I stopped singing soprano at choir practice.

When I taught piano, I sat three feet back and held a tissue over my mouth whenever I spoke.

Then in May, my nine-year-old student Leo finished his lesson.

His mother, Naomi, came inside to pick him up.

Naomi works as a registered dental hygienist at a periodontal clinic over by the medical center.

I was sitting on the bench wincing because my palate was throbbing. When Leo went to tie his shoes, Naomi stayed by the piano.

"Loretta," she said quietly. "Are you in pain? You have been pulling back and covering your mouth all afternoon."

I broke down right there.

I told her about Brenda and Ashley whispering behind my shoulder at the wedding.

I told her about the sour smell, the burning palate, and the gummy yellow sludge that neither brushes nor tablets could touch.

Naomi did not look disgusted.

"Loretta, you did not do anything wrong," she said. "Almost every denture wearer goes through this because nobody explains how acrylic actually works."

She told me dental acrylic is like a hard sponge, covered in millions of microscopic pores.

When adhesive paste mixes with saliva and food, it turns into a dense, sticky glue called biofilm.

Fizzy tablets cannot break down that glue, brushing only scratches the surface, and yeast feeds on it day and night.

"In professional dental labs, we never use fizzing tablets," Naomi said. "We clean appliances in clinical ultrasonic baths. High-frequency sound waves blast the biofilm out of the pores, and ultraviolet light kills the yeast."

I told her about the cheap $30 Amazon pod that died.

Naomi shook her head. "Those cheap ones are buzzing toys. They do not have true cavitation power and they do not have medical-grade UV-C light. If you want clinical-grade cleaning at home, choose an ultrasonic cleaner that is dentist-recommended."

She pulled out her phone and showed me the DentalPULSE Pod.

"This is the DentalPULSE Pod," Naomi said. "It uses genuine 42,000 Hz ultrasonic waves and medical-grade UV-C light together in one single 5-minute cycle. You do not buy tablets. You do not buy chemicals. You just use plain tap water."

I was skeptical.

When she said it cost $99.95, I hesitated.

Then Naomi looked at me. "Loretta, you spend $200 a year on tablets that do not work, and a replacement denture plate costs $1,500 to $4,000 if bacteria and fungus ruin the acrylic. A one-time purchase of $99.95 that actually cleans it with tap water costs way less."

I went home and ordered it that evening.

When it came, the DentalPULSE Pod had a solid stainless steel basin inside, a sleek lid with a built-in UV-C light window, and one single button on the front.

I filled the tank with plain lukewarm tap water.

No tablets.

No vinegar.

I dropped my upper plate into the water, closed the lid, and pressed the button.

A soft hum started, and a pale purple light began to glow through the window.

Exactly 5 minutes later, it clicked off.

I lifted the lid and gasped: the water was murky, cloudy brown, with flakes of yellowish gray sludge and old dissolved adhesive floating on top.

That was what had been sitting on the roof of my mouth every single day.

I rinsed the plate and slid it in.

Day 1: It felt completely different.

It did not feel slippery or greasy.

It felt as smooth and clean as polished glass against my tongue, with zero sour chemical smell.

Day 3: The cloudy white film in the molar crevices was completely gone.

It stopped yellow stains from setting into the edges of the artificial gumline, leaving the plate clean and clear again.

Day 7: The fiery red spots on the roof of my mouth were gone.

My palate was pale, healthy pink again, and the burning was completely gone.

Day 14: I wore my plate from seven in the morning until nine at night, through two choir rehearsals and four piano lessons.

When I popped it out before brushing, I sniffed the inside surface.

Nothing.

Not a trace of sewer odor, old paste, or sour yeast.

Then came Memorial Day weekend.

My son Brian hosted our annual family barbecue at his house up in the Sandia foothills.

About thirty relatives were there, sitting on patio chairs under the pinon trees.

Brenda and Ashley were already standing by the buffet table, dishing up potato salad and brisket.

Two months earlier, I would have stayed across the yard or hidden behind a potted plant.

Not this time.

I wore my favorite turquoise blouse and silver earrings.

I walked straight across the lawn and right up to the buffet table.

"Brenda, Ashley, how are you both doing?" I said, smiling wide.

Brenda turned around, and I saw her eyes flick to my mouth.

She stiffened a little, preparing for the smell she had mocked at the wedding.

I did not take a step back.

I leaned right in, close enough that our shoulders were touching over the salad bowls.

I laughed, asked about Ashley's job in Santa Fe, and talked about my spring piano recital.

Brenda blinked.

Her expression changed from guarded to completely confused.

At one point, she actually leaned in half an inch closer, her nose twitching just a fraction, trying to catch whatever she thought was coming.

There was nothing to smell except the grilled brisket and my iced tea.

Ashley looked at me and said, "Loretta, you look wonderful. You seem so energetic today."

"I feel wonderful, honey," I said, leaning in and giving Ashley a warm, full hug.

Ashley hugged me back tight, resting her chin right next to my cheek, without taking a single step backward.

Brenda stood there holding her paper plate, completely quiet.

She cleared her throat, looked down at her potato salad, and said, "That turquoise is a very nice color on you, Loretta."

"Thank you, Brenda," I said, smiling right into her eyes.

She did not say another word about my teeth, and she never whispered behind my shoulder again.

The vindication of that moment was worth a hundred times what I paid for that little machine.

I never had to worry about what someone was thinking when I opened my mouth ever again.
