## PRIMARY TEXT

My entire job for sixteen years has been hunting invisible bacteria in a regional hospital in Baton Rouge.

I swab bed rails, call buttons, and IV poles until my knuckles crack.

I audit hand-washing stations and track infection rates on pulmonary floors.

Yet back in September, I almost let a dirty piece of pink acrylic kill my mother.

Her name is Eloise.

She is eighty-four years old, a retired seamstress who spent forty years tailoring suits and hemming wedding gowns.

She lives with me in our house off Perkins Road.

For the past twelve years, she has worn a full set of upper and lower dentures.

She was always meticulous with them.

Every night before bed, she dropped them into a bathroom cup with warm water and a blue Polident tablet.

The water would fizz and turn bright blue.

Every morning, she stood over the sink and scrubbed them with a stiff denture brush until they smelled like wintergreen.

To anyone looking, she was doing everything right.

I thought so too.

Sure, over the past few years, I noticed stubborn yellow stains creeping into the crevices along the gumline.

The pink acrylic had turned cloudy.

Whenever she took them out, a faint sour smell lingered by the sink.

She would just wave her hand.

"Old plastic gets yellow, baby. That is just what happens."

I let it go.

That was the biggest mistake of my life.

Late August hit, right in the thick of Louisiana hurricane season.

It was muggy and suffocating.

My daughter Sylvie was getting ready for her senior year of high school.

Sylvie had just been nominated for the homecoming court for the big game in late October.

Mom was thrilled.

She was already pulling out her sewing machine to alter Sylvie's white homecoming dress.

Then, during the first week of September, Mom caught a mild stomach bug.

Just a little nausea and fatigue.

She rested in bed for a day and drank ginger ale.

By Wednesday, she seemed fine.

By Friday night, everything collapsed.

I walked into her bedroom at 10:00 PM to bring her a fresh glass of water.

Mom was shivering under three quilts, teeth chattering so hard they clicked.

Her forehead was burning.

My clinical thermometer read 103.4 degrees.

Then I heard her breathing.

It was a rapid, shallow rattle deep inside her chest.

When she tried to speak, she could barely pull enough air to say my name.

Her lips had a faint bluish tint.

I threw a coat over her shoulders, helped her into the passenger seat, and drove straight to the hospital ER where I work.

Within thirty minutes, the chest X-ray was up on the screen.

A dense, white consolidation filled the lower right lobe of her lung.

The ER physician looked at me with that grave look you never want to see from a colleague.

"Severe aspiration pneumonia, Camille. We are admitting her to the intensive care unit."

They put her on high-flow nasal oxygen.

They started IV vancomycin and cefepime to fight off sepsis.

For two days, I sat in a hard vinyl chair beside her bed in my scrubs.

I held her hand and listened to the hiss of the oxygen.

I was furious with myself.

I am an infection-prevention nurse.

I teach classes on cross-contamination.

How had my mother developed a life-threatening lung infection in our home?

At 2:00 AM on Sunday, Antoine, one of our senior respiratory therapists, came in to adjust Mom's oxygen flow.

Antoine has worked our pulmonary floor for nineteen years.

He looked at my mother sleeping.

Then his eyes drifted to the bedside stand.

Sitting right next to her water cup was her plastic denture bath, her false teeth resting in water.

Antoine pointed at the cup.

"Camille, how does she clean those at home?"

"Polident tablets every night," I whispered. "And she brushes them every morning."

Antoine shook his head and pulled up a stool.

"Camille, nobody ever swabs the teeth," he said quietly.

"What do you mean?"

"Acrylic is a porous resin," Antoine said. "Under a microscope, it looks like a sponge. It is full of microscopic pits, tunnels, and fissures."

"Effervescent tablets only fizz on the surface," he explained. "They give off a mint scent, but they cannot penetrate deep pores. That is where bacterial biofilm builds an impenetrable fortress."

Then Antoine quoted clinical studies that I should have known by heart.

He cited O'Donnell et al. 2016 in the Journal of Prosthodontics.

Researchers sampled dentures from elderly hospital patients and found pneumonia-causing pathogens on about 64% of them.

Nearly two out of three.

He cited Quinn et al. 2014 in the Journal of Nursing Scholarship.

A hospital system added basic daily oral care and cut non-ventilator hospital pneumonia by about a third, a 37% drop.

He cited Iinuma et al. 2015 in the Journal of Dental Research.

524 seniors aged eighty-five and older were followed for three years.

Those who slept in dentures had more than twice the pneumonia risk, along with heavy denture plaque and Candida fungal growth.

"When your mom got that stomach bug, her swallow reflex slowed down," Antoine told me.

"When an older person sleeps, saliva pools along the acrylic plate. Millions of trapped bacteria from those denture pores slip down the throat and straight into the lungs."

He paused.

"The denture was the reservoir. The denture gave her aspiration pneumonia."

The weight of his words hit me like a physical blow.

I blamed that dirty plastic plate outright.

The bacteria that nearly killed my mother had been multiplying in a cup on her bathroom counter every night.

Mom spent six days in the hospital before her fever broke and her oxygen stabilized.

I swore those teeth would never go back into her mouth covered in bacteria.

The problem was getting them clean.

First, I tried scrubbing them myself with a stiff brush.

All that did was create visible micro-scratches, giving bacteria and yellow stains more grooves to hide in.

Next, I tried soaking them in white vinegar.

It smelled like a pickle jar, tasted awful, and left the cloudy film intact.

A drop of bleach made the pink acrylic look chalky and brittle.

Then I bought a cheap $30 ultrasonic pod from an Amazon seller.

It was a flimsy plastic tub.

It buzzed loudly, rattled across the counter, and barely rippled the water.

It had no UV light.

After twenty-three days, the motor died completely.

I later found a ninety-day laboratory test of seven leading ultrasonic cleaners.

Six out of seven machines left live bacterial biofilm active on appliances overnight.

They clean for a week, the ultrasonic soundwaves weaken, and most quit before ninety days.

Meanwhile, Polident tablets cost $15 a box, a recurring cost you pay for forever on a method that only does half the job.

Plus, back in 2008, the FDA issued a public health notice on allergic reactions to persulfates used in those tablet cleansers.

I was at my wit's end.

A week later, I saw Antoine in the cafeteria.

"Did you get the DentalPULSE Pod yet?" he asked.

They blast deep into the microscopic pores and crevices where bristles and tablet fizz cannot reach.

Paying $99.95 one time for an ultrasonic cleaner that eliminates 99.9% of bacteria was an obvious choice.

I ordered the DentalPULSE Pod that afternoon.

When it came, that evening, I tested it.

I filled the stainless steel tank to the line with plain tap water from the sink.

Mom had just soaked her dentures in a Polident tablet an hour earlier, so they looked "clean" to the naked eye.

I placed both plates into the tank, put the lid on, and pressed the button.

The machine gave a steady, quiet hum.

Through the window, I could see the blue UV-C light glowing.

The cycle ran for exactly 5 minutes.

When the timer stopped, I lifted the lid.

What I saw inside made my stomach turn.

The water was no longer clear tap water.

It was murky, yellowish-brown, and cloudy.

Tiny floating specks of dark debris and yellowish film drifted in the basin.

Mom walked in, looked over my shoulder, and gasped.

"Was that inside my teeth?" she asked.

"Yes, Mom," I said. "That was living deep inside the pores."

That murky brown water was the bacterial biofilm and gunk that twelve years of tablets and brushing had left behind.

From that night on, the 5-minute cycle became Mom's evening routine.

The results came fast.

By Day 3, the cloudy white film on the palate plate was completely gone.

The acrylic felt smooth to her fingers.

By Week 2, Mom told me the teeth felt completely different in her mouth.

"They feel like smooth glass. No slimy feeling on the roof of my mouth, and no sour taste when I wake up."

The stubborn yellow stains along the gumline stopped spreading, and the acrylic looked clear again, not milky.

By late October, the moment we had waited for arrived.

It was a cool Friday night in Baton Rouge.

Sylvie was on the football field in her white dress on the homecoming court, smiling from ear to ear.

Right beside me in the bleachers, cheering at the top of her lungs, was my mother.

She was healthy, radiant, and full of life.

The next Sunday, Mom cooked a huge pot of chicken and sausage gumbo for the whole family, eating crusty French bread without hesitation.

Her lungs are completely clear.

She has not had a single fever or cough since.
