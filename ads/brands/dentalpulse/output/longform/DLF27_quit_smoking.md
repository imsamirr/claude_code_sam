## PRIMARY TEXT

I quit smoking in October of 2019 so I would stop getting chest infections.

By February of 2021, I was sitting on an exam table in the urgent care on Gloucester Crossing with pneumonia for the second winter in a row.

I was 49 years old.

I run a commercial lobster boat out of Gloucester, Massachusetts.

If you have ever worked the North Shore in January, you know what winter on the water is like.

You are down at the dock at 4:30 in the morning.

The deck has a thin skin of frozen salt spray.

The wind howls out of the northeast off Cape Ann, cutting straight through your bibs.

You haul 800 traps a day in six-foot swells, stacking wire gear, banding claws, breathing diesel fumes and freezing ocean mist.

It is hard work on a good day.

If your lungs go down, you are done.

That was why I quit smoking in 2019.

I went cold turkey after twenty-four years of a pack a day.

I did it for my lungs.

I started running three miles along Western Avenue every week.

I took vitamin C and ate clean.

I did everything right.

And then, every winter like clockwork, my chest gave out.

In December of 2019, it was a hacking cough that turned into severe bronchitis.

In January of 2021, my fever spiked to 102.4, my ribs ached, and I ended up flat on my back with bacterial pneumonia.

I missed thirteen days during peak holiday prices.

When winter lobster is bringing nine dollars a pound at the dock, sitting on the couch coughing up thick yellow gunk means burning through your mortgage money.

I sat in my truck in the harbor parking lot for twenty minutes staring at the steering wheel.

My sternman, a 26-year-old kid who smokes a pack of Camels on every trip, was out there pulling traps in thirty-knot winds without a runny nose.

And here I was, the guy who ran three miles before sunrise, wheezing into an inhaler.

I thought I was just unlucky.

Then my wife Colleen sat down at the kitchen table on a Sunday afternoon.

She had an accordion folder out with four years of our medical co-pays and pharmacy receipts.

She tapped her glasses against the table and looked right at me.

"Brendan, look at these dates."

From 2014 to 2019, I did not have a single urgent care visit for my chest.

Not one antibiotic prescription.

Then she slid a dental receipt across the table from November 12, 2019.

It was for my hard acrylic night guard.

My dentist had made it for me because twenty-five years of clenching my jaw through nor'easters had worn my back molars down to the dentin.

My first trip to urgent care for a chest infection was on January 4, 2020.

Almost six weeks after I started wearing that night guard.

Every single winter illness over the next three years happened between December and March, right in the middle of night guard season.

Everyone gives you the standard winter fisherman advice:

Drink hot black tea before you leave the harbor.

Wear double-layered merino wool under your bibs.

Take vitamin D3 every morning.

Keep your head dry when the sea kicks up.

Nobody ever talks about the five-inch piece of hard acrylic you shove into your mouth right before you close your eyes.

Two days later, I went back to the clinic for a chest x-ray.

My old deckhand Neil has a wife named Lauren who works there as a respiratory therapist.

She was standing at the counter talking to Dr. Hayes when I walked in.

I had my plastic night guard case in my jacket pocket, and when I pulled my truck keys out, the case fell and hit the floor.

Lauren looked at it.

"Brendan, what is that?"

"My night guard," I said. "For teeth grinding."

"How do you clean it?"

"I rinse it in the sink under hot tap water in the morning. Brush it with my toothbrush sometimes. Once a week I drop a blue tablet in a cup."

Lauren did not even smile.

She looked at Dr. Hayes, and Dr. Hayes shook his head.

"Brendan," Dr. Hayes said, "have you ever heard of aspiration pneumonia?"

"Wait, what does that even mean?" I asked.

Dr. Hayes pointed at the little plastic case.

"To your eye, that acrylic looks smooth and solid. But under a laboratory microscope, dental acrylic is full of microscopic pores, micro-fissures, and tiny scratches."

He told me that every night you wear it, your mouth bacteria, yeast, and saliva seep deep into those pores.

Then you take it out in the morning, give it a quick five-second splash under the tap, and snap it shut inside a dark, damp plastic case.

That closed case sits on your bathroom counter for sixteen hours at room temperature.

It is a warm incubator.

The bacteria and mold do not wash off with water.

They multiply inside the microscopic pores all day long.

And then at night, you put that bacteria-soaked piece of plastic right back over your teeth.

For eight hours straight, you swallow saliva and breathe shallowly over that colony.

When you sleep, your cough and swallow reflexes relax.

Micro-droplets of saliva carrying that live bacterial biofilm slip straight past your vocal cords and drain down into your bronchial tubes and your lungs.

It is called aspiration.

And your lungs are warm, dark, and moist, which means those mouth germs take root immediately.

I stood there in the clinic hallway staring at the linoleum floor.

Later on, I looked up the actual clinical research.

A 2015 study by Iinuma and colleagues in the Journal of Dental Research followed 524 people aged 85 and older for three years.

They found that seniors who slept in their dentures had more than double the risk of pneumonia compared to those who took them out.

They also had significantly more bacterial plaque and Candida yeast.

Another study in 2016 by O'Donnell in the Journal of Prosthodontics found that pneumonia-causing germs were on the dentures of about 64% of elderly hospital patients sampled.

The studies were on dentures, but the connection hit me like a rogue wave.

Dentures and night guards are molded from the exact same porous dental acrylic.

They sit in the same dark, humid plastic box all day, and you sleep over them all night.

And I had been soaking mine in spit, sealing it in a case, and breathing over it every single night.

I threw the plastic case onto the counter and paced the kitchen.

I had spent thousands on healthy food, running shoes, and gym visits, while shoving a concentrated ball of bacteria into my mouth seven nights a week.

I went home determined to clean it.

First, I scrubbed it with my regular soft toothbrush and paste.

The toothpaste cost five dollars.

It smelled like mint, but within two days the acrylic was getting cloudier.

Lauren told me that regular toothpaste contains microscopic abrasives made for enamel.

On acrylic, toothpaste gouges microscopic scratches into the surface, creating deeper trenches for yellow bacteria and white film to hide.

Next, I bought blue fizzing cleaning tablets from the grocery store.

That was eighteen dollars a month, or over two hundred dollars a year.

They bubbled in a cup and made my bathroom smell like pool chlorine.

The persulfates in those tablets burned my tongue in the morning.

In fact, the FDA had issued a public health notice about allergic reactions to persulfates in cleansers back in 2008.

Worst of all, the tablets only bleached the surface.

Underneath, the night guard still had a sour, stale smell, and the yellow stains around the molar channels kept getting darker.

Then I bought one of those $35 generic vibrating cleaning pods off Amazon.

It felt like a cheap plastic toy.

It buzzed loudly on the counter, but the water barely moved.

After nineteen days, the motor died completely.

I found out that in a 90-day lab test comparing seven leading ultrasonic cleaners, six out of seven pods left live bacterial biofilm active on appliances overnight.

They simply lacked the power to penetrate the pores.

A week later, Neil walked down our dock with a plain cardboard box under his arm.
