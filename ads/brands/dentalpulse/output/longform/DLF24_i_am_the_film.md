## PRIMARY TEXT

I live on the pink acrylic roof of Calvin's mouth.

To Calvin, I don't exist.

He is seventy years old, a retired Amtrak conductor living in Charleston, West Virginia. He spent forty-two years keeping passenger trains running on time on the Cardinal line through the New River Gorge, between Washington and Chicago. He worked through blizzards, rockslides, and forty-two Appalachian winters without missing a call. He is a man of strict habits and iron routines.

Every single night at 9:45 PM, Calvin takes his upper denture out, stands in front of the bathroom mirror, and drops a blue Polident tablet into a plastic cup of warm water.

The water turns bright blue. It fizzes. Hundreds of tiny effervescent bubbles rush to the surface, smelling like artificial peppermint and laundry bleach.

Calvin looks down at the fizz, smiles to himself in the mirror, clicks his tongue, and turns off the bathroom light.

He thinks those little bubbles are killing me.

He thinks that blue cup is keeping him clean and protected.

I laugh every single night.

He has no idea that those bubbles cannot even touch me.

To the human eye, Calvin's denture looks like a smooth piece of solid pink plastic. But to me and billions of my brothers, that acrylic is an endless mountain range of microscopic caves, tunnels, and deep craters.

Under a laboratory microscope, medical acrylic is as porous as a kitchen sponge.

When Calvin drops his teeth into that fizzy blue cup, the bubbles only wash over the outer surface. That fizz lasts three minutes. It bleaches the exterior, loosens a few stray food particles, and dies.

Meanwhile, I am tucked half a millimeter deep inside the microscopic pores of the acrylic. Water surface tension prevents the chemicals from ever reaching my home.

I don't just survive his nightly tablet soak. I thrive on it. I feed on the dead saliva proteins left behind on the surface.

Then comes morning.

At 7:00 AM, Calvin takes his denture out of the blue water and scrubs it with a stiff $4 denture brush and a ribbon of paste.

I love that brush.

Every time Calvin drags those stiff nylon bristles across the plate, he gouges millions of microscopic scratches into the soft acrylic.

You cannot see them with the naked eye. But to bacteria, every microscopic scratch is a brand new canyon. Every stroke of his brush carves more caves for my colony to expand. He thinks he is scrubbing me away. He is literally building me more real estate.

By 11:00 AM, Calvin's body heat warms the acrylic plate up to 98.6 degrees.

That is when we go to work.

We metabolize the saliva and microscopic food particles trapped deep in the pores. As we feed, we pump out volatile sulfur gases.

Calvin cannot smell it. His nose has been living with it for so long that his brain completely tunes it out. He keeps a roll of wintergreen Life Savers in his flannel shirt pocket, popping them after lunch, but mint doesn't cover sulfur. It just smells like rotting sugar.

And Peggy smells every bit of it.

Peggy has been married to Calvin for forty-five years.

On Sunday mornings before church on Virginia Street, Calvin always pours two cups of black coffee and leans across the kitchen counter to kiss Peggy good morning.

Three years ago, Peggy started flinching.

Now, whenever Calvin leans in, Peggy reflexively turns her cheek, pulls her chin back two inches, and holds her breath until he steps away.

Calvin pretends not to notice. But his shoulders drop every single time. He stopped kissing her on the lips years ago. When their daughter brings the grandkids over for Sunday roast, Calvin sits at the far end of the table. When someone tells a funny joke, he covers his mouth with his fist when he laughs.

He knows something is wrong.

He can see the dull, cloudy white film coating the crevices of his plate that never washes off. He can see the yellow stains setting in along the fake gumline that no amount of scrubbing will lift. And he can feel the burning, raw red patch on the roof of his mouth where our biofilm presses against his palate all day long.

Last November, Calvin even brought it up during his checkup at the dental clinic on Kanawha Boulevard.

The dentist took one look at Calvin's inflamed, red palate, shook his head, and warned him: "Calvin, you have got to stop using those abrasive paste scrubs and blue tablets. The acrylic is porous. You are just driving the bacteria deeper into the plate."

Did Calvin listen?

Of course not. He was seventy years old. He had been dropping a tablet in a cup for thirty years. He walked straight into the pharmacy that afternoon and bought another double pack.

Calvin tried all his own desperate fixes:

He bought double boxes of blue tablets ($18 a box, over $75 a year, year after year). He dropped two tablets in the glass instead of one. The fizz was louder. The water was darker blue. But water tension still kept it on the surface. We slept right through it.

He soaked his teeth in white vinegar and drugstore mouthwash ($5 a bottle). The vinegar made the bathroom smell like a pickle jar and turned his soft liner brittle. The mouthwash burned his gums and only masked our sulfur gases for twenty minutes.

He tried baking soda and dish soap. That only left a greasy film on the plate and tasted like chemicals for hours.

Then Calvin saw an ad on Facebook and bought a cheap $30 plastic cleaner.

He was so excited the day it arrived. He filled it with water, dropped his teeth in, and turned it on.

It buzzed on the counter like an angry electric razor.

The water barely rippled. To us, it felt like a gentle vibration. It didn't pull a single cell out of the acrylic pores. After twelve days, the cheap motor inside burned out, smelled like melted plastic, and stopped working completely. Calvin tossed it into the bottom bathroom drawer with his old shoe polish.

We felt completely untouchable.

We owned Calvin's denture. We owned his breath. We owned the space between him and his wife.

Then, on a Tuesday afternoon three weeks ago, a white box arrived on Calvin's porch in Charleston.

His daughter in Charlotte had sent it.

Inside was a device called the DentalPULSE Pod. An ultrasonic cleaner.

The moment Calvin opened the box, our reign was over.

At first, we weren't worried. We thought it was just another cheap plastic gimmick.

Then Calvin pulled it out of the packaging.

It was solid. Heavy. Inside was a smooth medical-grade stainless steel basin. On the front was a single button.

Calvin didn't reach for the blue tablets. He didn't pour in any chemicals.

He just filled the stainless steel tank with plain warm tap water from the bathroom faucet.

He placed his upper denture into the water. He clicked the lid shut.

And then Calvin pressed the button.

In an instant, everything changed.

A high-frequency hum tore through the water.

Forty-two thousand sound waves per second.

42,000 Hz.

This was not the gentle buzzing of his old $30 toy. This was pure ultrasonic cavitation.

At 42,000 Hz, the water inside the basin was ripped apart, creating millions of microscopic vacuum bubbles that violently imploded directly against the acrylic surface.

The force was savage.

Microscopic shockwaves blasted directly into the deepest pores of the acrylic. They flushed water in and out of caverns we had lived in since 2018.

Our sticky biofilm was ripped off the acrylic walls.

The suction yanked billions of us out of our tunnels and tore us into the water.

And right above us, through the clear window, the blue glow of a medical-grade UV-C light washed through the basin.

The UV-C light shattered our DNA instantly.

In one single 5-minute cycle, our entire colony was destroyed.

When the timer clicked off, Calvin walked over and lifted the lid.

The water was no longer clear.

It was a cloudy, murky, disgusting brownish-gray.

Calvin stared into that stainless steel tank with his mouth hanging open.

That brown cloud in the water was us.

Seven years of trapped biofilm, rotting sulfur bacteria, embedded food particles, and hidden gunk that his blue fizzy tablets had danced over every night, floating dead in plain tap water.

And that was my last breath.
