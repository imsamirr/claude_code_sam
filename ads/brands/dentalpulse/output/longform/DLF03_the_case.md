## PRIMARY TEXT

My husband found black spots inside my night guard case.
Packed into the hinge.
The one place I never looked.
For two years I took my guard out of that case and put it in my mouth every single day.
And for most of those two years I woke up with a raw throat, a musty taste and a cough nobody could explain.
I work in an ICU.
I of all people should have known.
I'm going to tell you what we found, what I still can't prove, and the one thing I stopped doing.

Let me back up.

My name is Dana. I'm 42.
I've been a unit clerk in a medical ICU outside Pittsburgh for eleven years.
Nights. 7 p.m. to 7:30 a.m.
I'm the voice on the unit phone at 3 a.m. when a family calls to ask how their mom is doing.

I also grind my teeth.
Hard.
My dentist says night shift does that to people.
Two years ago she made me a custom night guard.
$600.

I call it a night guard, but I sleep from 8:30 in the morning to 3 in the afternoon.
It goes in every time I sleep.

Here's how I took care of it.
Rinse under the tap when I woke up.
Brush it with my toothbrush and toothpaste most days.
On weekends, an Efferdent tablet in a cup.
Then back in its little blue plastic case.
Snap. Into my work bag, into my locker for a 12-hour shift, then back in my mouth.

That's what the case is for, right?

About four months in, the mornings started.
My mornings are 3 p.m., but you know what I mean.

I'd wake up with my throat scratchy, like I'd been breathing dust all day.
And a taste on my tongue I can only describe as basement.
Musty. Damp. Old.
Plus a dry little cough that hung around until my first coffee and came back at 2 a.m. at the desk.

I went to urgent care.
Allergies, the PA said.
Post-nasal drip.
"Half of Pennsylvania has it," she said.
Allergy pills. A nasal spray.

Nothing changed.

I bought a $70 humidifier. New pillows. A $40 air purifier filter.
Lozenges in every pocket of my scrubs.

One January the cough went wet and I got my first round of antibiotics.
It cleared for about three weeks.
Then the throat came back. Then the taste.
Then a second round of antibiotics in April.
"Some people just have sensitive airways," the doctor said.
"Keep an eye on it."

It took three things from me.

The phone.
Families call the ICU at all hours. Scared people.
One night a woman called about her father.
I coughed in the middle of a sentence. Twice.
There was a pause on the line.
Then she said, "Are you sick? Should you even be in there with him?"
I told her I was fine.
After that I cleared my throat before every call.
I rehearsed hello.

Sunday coffee.
Marcus works days. I work nights.
The one hour we really get is Sunday morning when I get home.
Two mugs on the back porch before I go to bed.
Nine years of that.
Last year I started skipping it. My throat hurt. I just wanted to lie down.
He'd sit out there alone.

And the kiss.
I stopped kissing Marcus when I woke up.
Because of the taste.
I'd turn my face so he got my cheek.
He never said a word.
I think he thought it was him.

Then came the night with the mask.

February. 4:10 a.m.
I was paging a resident and started coughing and couldn't stop.
Eyes watering. Bent over the counter.
Tonya, our charge nurse, set a mask on the desk in front of me.
"Put that on, honey. We've got two fresh transplants tonight."
She was right. That was the worst part.
I wore the mask the rest of the shift and cried in my car in the parking garage before I drove home.

Marcus was up when I walked in.
I told him about the mask, and how tired I was of tasting basement.
He went quiet. Then he asked, "When's the last time you cleaned the case?"

I looked at him.
The case?
I cleaned the guard. Every day.
The case was just the case.

He picked it up off the bathroom counter, opened it and held his phone flashlight right on the hinge.

Black specks.
Packed into the hinge.
A line of them along the rim where the lid snaps shut.
A gray, slick smear in one corner of the bottom.

"That's the same stuff that was in the washer," he said.

He meant our front-load washer.
Last fall he pulled back the rubber seal on the door and found it lined with black mold.
The seal stays wet. The door stays shut. It's dark in there.
Mold doesn't need much more than that.

I held the case up to my nose.
I had never once smelled it on purpose.
It smelled exactly like the taste in my mouth.

I'm not going to pretend I didn't gag.

Here's the part that still keeps me up.

On my unit, patients on breathing machines get their mouths cleaned on a schedule.
Every few hours.
Swabs. Suction. A soft brush.
I've filed those sheets for eleven years.
Do you know why that protocol exists?
Because germs that live in the mouth can slide down into the lungs.
In a person who can't cough them back up, that can turn into pneumonia.
Every ICU in the country fights it. There's a whole name for it on the ventilator.

I knew all of that.
I just never once thought about what I was putting in my own mouth.

So I did the math I should have done two years earlier.

My guard was in my mouth about 8 hours a day.
It sat in that case the other 16.
Wet, because I rinsed it and snapped it straight in.
Closed, because that's what a case does.
Dark.
Warm, in a bag, in a locker, in a hospital kept at 72 degrees all night.

16 hours a day.
Wet. Closed. Dark. Warm.
That's not storage.
That's an incubator.
That's my washer seal.

Yeast, mold and bacteria grow in exactly that.
Then every day I pressed that guard over my teeth and breathed across it for 8 hours.
Mouth to throat. The whole time I slept.

And not one thing in my routine ever touched the case.

I want to be honest with you here.
I can't prove that case made me sick.
No doctor ever said, "It's your night guard."
Maybe it really was allergies the whole time.
I don't know.

But I knew one thing standing in that bathroom.
I was not putting that guard in my mouth that way one more day.

Marcus threw the case in the kitchen trash and tied the bag.
I bought a new one. Now it gets washed with dish soap and dries wide open on a towel. It only closes when it's bone dry.

The case was the easy part.
The guard had been living in that for two years.

At my next cleaning I brought it.
My hygienist turned it over under her light and made a face she tried to hide.
She put it in the ultrasonic machine in the back.
Sound waves in water make millions of tiny bubbles.
They pop against the plastic and knock the film out of scratches and grooves a brush can't reach.
"Problem is," she said, "most people only get this twice a year. Here."

Twice a year.
I sleep in it every day.

So I went looking for that at home.
Ultrasonic cleaning.
And UV-C light, the kind we use at work, because shaking germs loose isn't the same as killing them.

Five things. All five or nothing.

1. Real ultrasonic. Strong enough to reach into the scratches, not just jiggle the water.
2. Medical-grade UV-C, for what the bubbles knock loose.
3. Tap water only. No tablets. I was done trading a musty taste for a minty one.
4. Fast. I get home at 8 in the morning. 5 minutes, not 20.
5. A stainless steel basin. I'd had enough of plastic that holds onto things.

The $30 ones on Amazon fell off fast. No real UV-C. Plastic tubs. Reviews that said "died in a month."
Then I found a 90-day lab test of 7 leading ultrasonic cleaners, run side by side.
6 of 7 pods left live bacteria behind.
One came out on top.
It was the only one that checked all five of my boxes.

DentalPULSE.

I'll be honest. I thought it was a gadget.
I've watched a lot of gadgets come through a hospital.
$99.95 for a cleaner felt like a lot.
Marcus added it up on the back of an envelope.
Three urgent care copays. Two rounds of antibiotics. The humidifier. The filters. The nasal spray. The lozenges.
"You already spent more than this on the problem," he said.
"This is the first thing you're spending on the cause."

The first time, I'd already brushed the guard when I woke up.
Tap water to the line.
Guard in.
Lid on.
One button.
5 minutes.

The water went cloudy.
Little flecks floating in it.
From a guard I'd cleaned an hour earlier.
Marcus leaned over my shoulder and said, "Well."
That was all. Just, "Well."

That cloud was everything my routine had been leaving behind.
The rinse. Water alone doesn't lift a film.
The toothbrush. My hygienist told me toothpaste is gritty. It leaves tiny scratches in the plastic, and scratches are where the film hides.
The weekend tablet. Blue water, minty smell. It made the guard smell clean. It only ever worked on the surface.

Here's the honest part.
Week one, nothing.
My throat was still scratchy on days two, three and four.
I told Marcus maybe it really was allergies.

The routine was simple, so I kept it.
I wake up at 3.
Guard goes in the Pod while I brush my own teeth.
5 minutes.
It dries on a clean towel.
Into the clean, dry case.
Into my bag.

Day 10, I woke up at 3:15 and lay there for a minute.
Something was missing.
I ran my tongue over my teeth.
No basement.

Week three, I realized I hadn't touched a lozenge all shift.

Week four, the phone rang at 2 a.m.
A man asking about his wife.
I picked up on the first ring and said hello like a normal person.
No throat clearing. No rehearsing.
He thanked me for being so calm.
I didn't tell him what that meant to me.

Week five was a Sunday.
I walked in at 8 a.m. and didn't go to the bedroom.
I went to the back porch.
Two mugs.
Marcus looked up like I was a deer in the yard.

And that afternoon I woke up at 3, rolled over and kissed my husband.
On the mouth.
He laughed. Then he didn't say anything for a while.

Tonya asked me in April what I'd done different. I told her about the hinge.
She went home and checked hers. She wears a guard too.
She texted me one word at 9 a.m. "GROSS."
She has her own Pod now.

I can't tell you the Pod fixed my throat.
I changed two things at once, the case and the cleaning.
I'm a unit clerk, not a scientist.
What I can tell you is what's in my mouth now.
And what isn't.

Last Sunday Marcus picked up the new case off the counter.
Opened it.
Turned on his phone flashlight and held it on the hinge for a long time.
"Nothing," he said.
Then he handed me my coffee.
