## PRIMARY TEXT

There is a $4,200 set of custom teeth sitting on my bathroom counter in Billings, Montana right now.

For five months, they spent more time soaking in cold water than they ever spent inside my mouth.

Every morning, I would look at that plastic cup and shake my head.

Forty-two hundred dollars in hard cash, saved up after thirty-five years of pulling freight in an eighteen-wheeler between Chicago and Seattle.

When I retired at sixty-two, my natural teeth were shot from decades of black coffee and highway food.

I wanted to fix my smile.

I wanted to bite into a thick ribeye steak without wincing.

Most of all, I wanted to smile in pictures with my eight-year-old grandson Tyler without feeling ashamed.

So I went to a dental clinic in town and bought a full set of custom acrylic dentures.

They looked bright, white, and sharp.

I thought my problems were over.

I had no idea the nightmare was just getting started.

Three weeks after I started wearing them, the trouble began.

It started as a dull itch along the roof of my mouth.

Within forty-eight hours, that itch turned into a fiery, raw burn.

It felt like someone had pressed a hot clothes iron against my palate and held it there.

Taking the top plate out at night felt like peeling a bandage off a second-degree burn.

When I looked in the mirror with a flashlight, the entire roof of my mouth was bright crimson and swollen.

I could not wear the teeth for more than twenty minutes without tears coming to my eyes.

I could not chew solid food.

I lived on lukewarm canned tomato soup, mashed potatoes, and oatmeal.

The worst part was the isolation.

For twenty years, a group of retired drivers met every Tuesday morning at the 4B's diner on South 27th Street for breakfast.

We drank coffee, swapped road stories, and gave each other a hard time.

After my mouth caught fire, I stopped showing up.

I did not want my buddies seeing me sit there gumming a bowl of warm broth.

Then came Tyler's Saturday morning hockey games at the arena in Billings.

Winter in Montana gets brutally cold, ten below zero with the wind whipping off the plains.

When you sit in a freezing ice rink without your upper teeth, the cold air hits your bare gums like a knife.

I was too embarrassed to let my grandson see me looking like an old man who gave up.

So I made up excuses and stayed home alone.

I went back to the dentist.

He took one look at my palate and gave me the diagnosis: denture stomatitis.

He said it is a fungal and bacterial infection caused by Candida albicans yeast.

He prescribed nystatin oral rinse.

I swished that yellow liquid four times a day for two weeks.

The fiery redness cooled down.

My palate looked pink again.

I popped my $4,200 dentures back in, feeling relieved.

Ten days later, the fire came roaring back.

My palate was raw, bleeding at the margins, and burning worse than before.

I went back to the clinic.

Another doctor gave me a second round of antifungal rinse.

Same story.

It cleared up while I swished, and the second I put my teeth back in, the burn returned.

That is when I realized something horrifying.

The infection was not starting in my mouth.

It was living inside the dentures.

Every time I put that $4,200 piece of acrylic against the roof of my mouth, I was re-infecting myself.

I tried everything to sanitize those teeth.

First, I used Polident and Efferdent tablets.

I dropped two blue tablets in a glass every night.

The water bubbled, turned blue, and smelled like mint.

However, the next morning, when I slid the plate into my mouth, the burn started up again by noon.

Tablets only bubble on the surface.

They cost $15 a box, a recurring bill you pay forever, but they never kill the deep fungus.

Plus, back in 2008, the FDA issued a public health notice on persulfate allergies from denture tablets.

Next, I bought a hard-bristled denture brush and scrubbed them with dish soap.

I scrubbed until my knuckles were white.

All that did was carve microscopic scratches into the acrylic, creating even more crevices for bacteria and yellow stains to burrow into.

I tried soaking them in white vinegar.

It made the teeth taste like sour salad dressing and made my gums sting.

Then I bought a cheap $35 ultrasonic cleaner off Facebook.

It arrived in a cheap box.

The basin was flimsy plastic, not metal.

It buzzed like an angry mosquito, but it barely moved the water.

It had no UV light at all.

After eighteen days, the switch broke and the motor quit running.

I was out forty-two hundred dollars for teeth I could not wear, plus hundreds of dollars on rinses, tablets, and junk gadgets.

Those teeth sat in a glass on my counter like an expensive paperweight.

Then, two weeks before Thanksgiving, I went down to the VA clinic for a routine check.

While waiting in the lobby, I started talking to an older guy named Chet.

Chet was seventy-one, a veteran who had worked as a dental lab technician for thirty-eight years.

He literally built acrylic dentures for a living.

I ended up telling him about my burning mouth and how my teeth were living in a cup.

Chet shook his head.

"So you take your antifungal swish, clear the yeast off your gums, and then press that infected plate right back against your palate."

"You re-seed the yeast every single morning."
