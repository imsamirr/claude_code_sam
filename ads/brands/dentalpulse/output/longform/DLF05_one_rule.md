## PRIMARY TEXT

The day they pulled all my teeth, the dentist gave me one rule.
No more cleaning tablets.
Nobody told me what to use instead.

So for 90 days, I cleaned a brand-new $3,800 set of teeth with dish soap and a baby toothbrush.
I kept a diary the whole time.
I'm sharing it because of a little boy named Mason, and a question he asked me at 3:12 on a Tuesday afternoon.
And because of what a woman in a denture lab told me in week nine.
It's long. Please read it anyway.
If you just had your teeth out, this is the diary I wish someone had handed me.

Let me back up.

My name is Lisa. I'm 54.
I drive a school bus outside Springfield, Missouri.
Route 14. Nineteen years.
Forty-one kids every morning, kindergarten through eighth grade.
I know every name, and who forgot their lunch again.

My teeth were bad for a long time.
Gum disease runs in my family. My mom had dentures at 50.
I spent most of my forties smiling with my lips shut.

Every year on the last day of school, the drivers line up in front of the buses with our kids for a picture.
It hangs in the breakroom at the bus depot.
In last year's picture, I'm the only driver with her mouth closed.
Remember that picture. It matters later.

So this February, I did it.
All of them out in one day.
Immediate dentures. That means the new teeth go in the same day the old ones come out.
They put a soft liner inside, because your gums shrink and change for months.
$3,800. Our whole tax refund and a chunk of savings.
I told my husband, Dale, this was the year I'd be in that picture with my teeth showing.

Here's the diary.

DAY 1.
The dentist went over everything. Keep them in tonight. Salt water. Soft foods.
Then she said one thing twice.
"And no more cleaning tablets. They'll harden the soft liner."
I nodded.
I was too numb to ask the only question that mattered.
What do I use instead?

DAY 4.
I called the office.
The girl at the front desk said, "Just keep them clean."
Clean with what?
"Mild soap and a soft brush should be fine."
Should be.
So I bought a bottle of blue dish soap.
And the softest toothbrush in the store. It came from the baby aisle. It had a yellow duck on the handle. $2.49.

DAY 9.
First morning back on the route.
Mason gets on at my third stop. He's six.
He yelled from the top step, "Miss Lisa got NEW TEETH!"
The whole bus clapped.
I gave them the biggest smile of my life.
That night, scrubbing at the sink, I noticed the smell for the first time.
Faint. Like a dishrag left in the sink over a weekend.

WEEK 3.
Every night: dish soap, duck brush, rinse, glass of water.
Every morning: the same thing again.
The liner started to feel fuzzy. Little scuffs. A grayish shade at the edges.
The harder I scrubbed, the rougher it got.
I went online at 1 a.m.
One site said vinegar. One said never vinegar.
One said baking soda. One said it scratches.
Then I found a woman on a forum who wrote almost my exact words.
"My dentist did not explain a thing other than no more cleaning tablets."
Hundreds of people. Same rule. Same gap.

WEEK 5.
Two sore spots. Upper left. Lower right.
I went back. "That's normal with immediates. Your gums are shrinking."
They ground down the plate a little. Better for four days. Then back.
I stopped eating lunch in the depot breakroom with Rhonda and the other drivers.
Now I ate in my bus with the doors shut, so nobody would see me pop them out to rinse.
At home, I started sleeping facing the wall.
I stopped kissing Dale good morning.
He asked me once. I said I had a sore.
That was half true.

WEEK 7.
Tuesday. 3:12 p.m. Last stop.
Mason stood at the top of the steps with his backpack on.
"Miss Lisa, are you mad at us?"
"No, baby. Why would you think that?"
"You don't smile anymore."
Then he ran up his driveway.
I sat there with the engine running.
There's a big mirror over the windshield so we can see the kids.
That afternoon, I looked at me.
Lips pressed shut. The same face as last year's picture.
Three thousand eight hundred dollars, and I was right back where I started.

That night, 11:40, I was at the sink with the duck brush again.
Dale came in for a glass of water.
I held the plate up under the light.
There was a white coating on the liner. Patchy. Like the fuzz on old bread.
I looked at the roof of my mouth in the mirror.
Bright red. Burning. The exact shape of my plate.
Dale didn't say a word for a second.
Then he said, "Lise. Call them in the morning."

WEEK 8.
My dentist looked for a long time.
"This is the start of a yeast infection. It's called denture stomatitis."
Candida. Yeast.
She said it's common. Studies say up to about two-thirds of denture wearers get it.
Sleeping in your dentures and how you clean them are the two big risk factors.
I'd been sleeping in mine every night since day one, because I couldn't stand Dale seeing me without them.
Then she said the part I can't forget.
"Your soft liner is the softest, most porous material in your mouth. Yeast loves it."
She gave me a rinse. She told me to take the plate out at night.
And she said, "Keep that liner very clean."
I said, "With what? You told me no tablets."
She paused.
"Soap and water is fine."
The exact thing I'd been doing for eight weeks while this happened.
She sent me to the lab to get the liner replaced early.

WEEK 9. The lab.
The tech's name is Marisol. Thirty years making teeth. Reading glasses on top of her head.
She took one look at my old liner.
"Let me guess. Dish soap and a toothbrush."
How did you know?
"Because you're the third one this month. They tell you what not to use. Nobody tells you what to use."
Then she explained it in a way I finally understood.

"You have tile in your shower?"
Yes.
"The hard pink part of your denture is the tile. The soft liner is the grout."
"You can scrub tile clean. Grout is soft and full of tiny holes."
"Scrub grout with a brush and you rough it up. You make more holes. And the black stuff lives down inside them."
"Soap cleans the top. The yeast is underneath."
"A tablet would harden your grout and crack it. A brush wears it out. Neither one gets down in the holes."

So what gets down in the holes?
She pointed at a steel tank on her counter, humming.
"Sound."
Ultrasonic cleaning.
Millions of tiny bubbles that pop against every surface, even inside pores where no bristle fits.
"We run everything through it back here."
"But shaking it loose isn't the same as killing it. If I was you, I'd want UV-C light on top. That's the germ-killing kind."
Then she said, "Ask your dentist about your liner. Every liner is a little different."
(I did. Mine said tap water was fine.)

Before I left, she had me do one thing. You can do it tonight.
Take your plate out. Press your thumbnail into the liner near the back, hard, for three seconds.
Then smell your thumbnail.
"That's what your mouth has been sitting in all night," she said.
Mine smelled like the dishrag.
And I'd scrubbed it that morning.

That night, I wrote a list on the back of a route sheet.
1. Tap water only. Nothing that can harden my liner.
2. Ultrasonic, strong enough to get down in the holes.
3. Real UV-C light, for whatever the sound shakes loose.
4. No scrubbing. My liner had been scrubbed enough.
5. Fast. I'm out the door at 5:40 a.m.

The cheap ones on Amazon didn't have UV-C at all.
A couple of them wanted me to buy their tablets. Tablets!
One checked every box.

42,000 Hz of ultrasonic cleaning.
Medical-grade UV-C light.
One button. One 5-minute cycle.
Tap water only. No tablets, no chemicals, no subscription.
Stainless steel basin.
Dentist-recommended, rated 4.8 out of 5, and it eliminates 99.9% of bacteria in one cycle.
$99.95.

I almost didn't.
I'd already spent our tax refund on these teeth.
Dale looked at the receipt for the rinse, the duck brush, and the second liner on the counter.
He said, "Lise. You spent $3,800 on teeth you won't smile with. What's a hundred bucks?"
He was right. The rinse copay, the second liner, the duck brush, the soap. I'd already spent more than that being careful.
So I ordered it. And I promised myself I'd send it back if it was another gadget.

DAY 68.
5:20 a.m.
Tap water to the line. Plate in. One button.
It hums. Five minutes. Just long enough to fill my coffee thermos.
When I lifted the plate out, the water had gone cloudy.
Little white flecks floating in it.
From a liner I'd scrubbed with soap four hours before.
I did the thumbnail test right there at the counter.
Nothing. It smelled like nothing.

WEEK 10.
Honest week.
The sore spots were still sore. That was the rinse's job and my gums healing, not a machine's.
The routine was the thing that changed.
Plate out at night, in its case.
Pod every morning while the coffee drips.
No duck brush. No blue bottle. No 1 a.m. searching.
Five minutes, and the kitchen still smelled like coffee instead of dish soap.

DAY 75.
I ate lunch in the breakroom.
Turkey sandwich. Rhonda told the story about the bus that got stuck behind a cattle trailer.
I laughed so hard I had to put my sandwich down.
With my mouth open.

DAY 79.
I kissed Dale good morning in the kitchen.
He said, "Well. Hello there."
I've been sleeping facing him again.

DAY 83.
Rhonda caught me in the depot lot.
"Okay. What did you do? You look ten years younger."
I told her about Marisol and the grout.
Her sister in Joplin got immediates in April. Same one rule. Same dish soap.
Rhonda had her sister's address written on her hand before her afternoon route.

WEEK 12.
Checkup.
My dentist looked at the roof of my mouth and said, "That's pink. That's what we want to see."
She looked at the new liner. "This looks great. Whatever you're doing, keep doing it."
I want to be honest with you.
I can't tell you the Pod is what cleared my palate. The rinse did that, and taking the plate out at night.
What I can tell you is this.
My first liner was gray, fuzzy and smelled like a dishrag by week three.
The new one has been in my mouth 41 days.
It still smells like nothing.

DAY 90.
Last day of school.
Seventy-something degrees. The kids were wild. Water balloons in backpacks.
We lined up in front of the buses for the picture.
Mason pushed his way to the front and stood right in front of me.
He looked up and shouted, "Miss Lisa's SMILING!"
I was. Every tooth.
The picture's up in the breakroom now.
I'm the driver with the most teeth showing.

The blue dish soap is back by the kitchen sink, where it belongs.
The duck toothbrush is in a drawer. I keep it so I remember.
