## PRIMARY TEXT

I'm 37 years old.
Never smoked a cigarette in my life.
I had pneumonia twice last winter.
The second round put me in a hospital bed for three nights.
Nobody could tell me why.
Then at 2 in the morning a woman with a nebulizer asked me one question that none of my doctors had.
"Do you sleep with anything in your mouth?"
I did.
For four years.

Let me back up.

My name's Mike.
I run a framing crew outside Murfreesboro, Tennessee.
Five guys. I've been swinging a hammer since I was 19.
I don't get sick. Flu shot every year. Don't dip. I can still carry a bundle of shingles up a ladder without stopping.

Could.

I also grind my teeth.
Jen, my wife, says it sounds like somebody chewing gravel.
Four years ago I cracked a molar and the dentist made me a night guard.
$450.

Here's how I took care of it. Be honest about yours.
Out in the morning. Rinse under the tap. Into the case.
Case goes in the door pocket of my truck or my gym bag.
I dropped it in sawdust once. Blew it off. Put it in that night.
Every couple of weeks, if Jen bugged me, I'd brush it with toothpaste.
Once in a while an Efferdent tablet in a cup.
Four years.
I'd have told you that was clean.

December, I got sick.
Cough, fever, chest like somebody was standing on it.
Urgent care. Chest X-ray. Pneumonia.
"Young guy like you, you'll bounce back."
Antibiotics. Back on the job in a week. Coughed for a month.

Then February.
Same thing, worse.
I was setting rafters on a two-story in Smyrna and I couldn't get a breath at the top of the ladder.
Had to sit down on the top plate, twenty feet up.
My lead, Carlos, drove me home in my own truck.

That night my fever hit 103.
ER at 1 in the morning. Oxygen low. Admitted.
Pneumonia again. Both lungs this time.

Three nights.

Here's what that cost me.

The job.
When I'm out, my crew doesn't run.
The builder couldn't wait on me. He gave the next two houses to another crew.
I lost about $6,000. One of my guys didn't come back.

Kenzie.
My daughter's 9. First year of rec league basketball.
We'd practiced in the driveway every night since Thanksgiving.
Her first real game was the Saturday I was in that bed.
Jen held up her phone in the gym so I could watch.
Kenzie missed both her free throws.
And after each one she looked up into the bleachers.
For me.
I watched that on a phone with a tube in my nose.

And Jen.
She didn't sleep those three nights.
After I got home she'd lie awake listening to me breathe.
I caught her once at 3 a.m. with her hand flat on my back, counting.

The second night in the hospital is when it happened.

Around 2 a.m. a respiratory therapist came in for my breathing treatment.
Denise. Fifties, reading glasses on a cord.
She looked at my chart for a while.
Then she said, "Thirty-seven. Nonsmoker. Twice in one winter."
"Yes ma'am."
"Do you sleep with anything in your mouth?"

I almost laughed. Then I told her.

She asked how I cleaned it.
The tap. The case. The truck.
She took her glasses off.

When you sleep, your body turns way down.
Your swallow slows. Your cough reflex gets weak.
Everybody breathes in tiny bits of what's in their mouth at night.
Doctors call it micro-aspiration.
Most nights, healthy lungs clean it up and you never know.

"Now think about what you're putting in there," she said.
A plastic tray, sitting right on your teeth, for 8 hours.
You breathe across it all night.
If that tray is coated in bacteria, you just gave your lungs a bigger job.
Every night for four years.

It has a name. Aspiration pneumonia.
Germs from the mouth get breathed or slipped into the lungs, usually during sleep.
It's one of the leading causes of pneumonia in older adults.

"I'm 37," I said.

"I know," she said. "That's why I asked."

Then the research. I'll be straight, because she was.
The big studies are on dentures.
Not night guards.

In 2015, the Journal of Dental Research published a study of people 85 and older.
The ones who slept in their dentures had more than twice the pneumonia risk over three years.

In 2016, another study in the same journal swabbed the dentures of elderly hospital patients.
About 64% of those dentures carried germs that can cause pneumonia.

And back in 1999, a study in The Lancet looked at nursing homes in Japan.
Residents who got daily oral care had less pneumonia. About 11% against 19%.
And fewer of them died of it.

I'm not 85.
I don't wear dentures.
I know.

Here's what I kept thinking at 3 in the morning.
A denture is a piece of plastic that sits in your mouth all night.
So is mine.

Denise never said my guard gave me pneumonia.
Neither will I. Maybe it was bad luck.
But nobody had ever said one word to me about that piece of plastic.
Not my dentist. Not urgent care. Not the box of tablets.

Here's how she put it so it stuck.

You know a cooler left shut in the truck bed with an inch of melt water in it?
Open it a week later in July. That smell.
It's just wet, closed and dark, and stuff grows.

That was my case.
Wet guard, snapped shut, in a truck that hits 120 inside in the summer.
16 hours a day.
Then 8 hours in my mouth, with me breathing across it.

When I got home I did the test Denise told me about.
First thing in the morning, I wiped the inside of the guard with a plain white paper towel.
Hard. Like a dipstick.

The towel came back yellow.
Slick. Like grease off a hinge.
From a guard I'd rinsed the morning before.

Then I opened the case and smelled it.
That cooler.

I threw the case in the trash can in the garage.

My dentist's office cleans appliances in an ultrasonic machine.
Sound waves in water make millions of tiny bubbles that pop against the plastic and blast the film out of the scratches.
Add UV-C light, the germ-killing kind, and you kill what the bubbles knock loose.

I buy tools for a living. Cheap tools cost you twice.
So I shopped it like a tool.

1. Real ultrasonic power. Enough to get into the scratches, not just shake the water.
2. Medical-grade UV-C, so what comes loose doesn't just sit there.
3. Tap water only. No tablets to buy, no chemical taste.
4. One cycle, 5 minutes. I'm out the door at 5:30. Longer than that, I won't do it.
5. Built right. Steel basin, not a plastic tub that cracks.

The $30 ones online went first. No real UV. Plastic tanks. Reviews saying they quit in a week or two.
Then I found a 90-day lab test of 7 leading ultrasonic cleaners, side by side.
6 of 7 pods left live bacteria.
One passed.

DentalPULSE.

I'll be honest, I figured it was a gimmick.
$99.95 for something to clean a mouthguard?
Then I did the math on the back of a lumber invoice.
$6,000 in lost work. Two urgent care visits. An ER. Three nights in a hospital.
I'd paid $450 for the guard and zero dollars to keep it clean.
That's the whole problem right there.

The first morning I ran it, I'd already rinsed the guard like always.
Tap water in. Guard in. Lid. One button.
5 minutes.

The water came out cloudy.
Little bits floating in it.
Jen looked at it and said, "That's been in your mouth every night?"
Yes, ma'am.

Here's what I learned about my old cleaning, from Denise and then from my dentist when I finally asked.
Rinsing does nothing to a film. It slides right over it.
Toothpaste is gritty. It scratches the plastic, and scratches are where bacteria dig in where a brush can't reach.
The tablet fizzes on the surface. Smells minty. Doesn't get into the scratches.
That cloudy water was four years of that.

Now, the honest part.
Week one, I didn't feel a thing different.
I was already off the antibiotics and still coughing in the mornings.
The Pod didn't change that overnight, and I'm not going to tell you it did.

What changed was the guard.
The paper towel came back white.
Every morning.
I'd do it on the tailgate before work just to see.
White.

Week two, something I didn't expect.
I always woke up with a taste. Like a penny that sat in a wet glove.
I figured that was just mornings.
It was gone.
I hadn't known it was there until it wasn't.

Jen noticed something too. She stopped pulling the covers over her nose when I rolled over in the morning.
She only told me that later. She holds onto things.

The routine is dead simple.
Alarm at 5.
Guard comes out, goes in the Pod.
5 minutes while I make coffee.
It dries on the counter, wide open.
New case, dry, goes in the bathroom drawer.
Not the truck.

March 9th I was back on a roof.
Cold morning. Frost on the OSB.
I carried a full sheet up the ladder and didn't stop at the top.
Carlos clapped once, real slow.
It took me most of spring to win back the builder. He gave us a house in May.
We've had work ever since.

Kenzie had four games left in her season.
I made all four.
Front row of the bleachers, hollering like an idiot.
In the last one she went to the line twice.
Made the second one.
She looked straight up at me.
I was right there.

Jen sleeps through the night again.
No hand on my back.
No counting.

Carlos wears a guard too. Found that out in April.
I handed him a paper towel on the tailgate and didn't say a word.
He looked at it a long time.
He's got his own Pod now.

In May I drove back to the hospital and left a card for Denise at the respiratory desk.
I wrote one line in it.
"Nobody else asked."

I can't promise you anything about your lungs.
I'm a framer, not a doctor.
One clean winter doesn't prove a thing, and Denise would smack me if I said it did.
What I can tell you is what's on my guard now.
Nothing.

The truck door pocket holds a tape measure and a carpenter pencil.
That's all it holds.
