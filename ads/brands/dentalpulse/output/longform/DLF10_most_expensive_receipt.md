## PRIMARY TEXT

Retired accountants keep receipts.
The most expensive one in my shoebox is from the hospital.
It isn't the biggest number in there. Medicare saw to that.
It's the most expensive because of what it paid for.
Six nights in a vinyl chair, listening to my wife breathe.
And because of what I learned on the fourth day, from a young woman holding a spoonful of applesauce.
I'm going to give you the whole ledger.

Let me back up.

My name is Hal. I'm 72. I live in Mesa, Arizona.
I was a CPA for 38 years.
I have kept every receipt since 1979, in shoeboxes, by year, in the hall closet.
My wife June says I'm the only man in Arizona who can tell you what he paid for a toaster in 1994.
It was $18.99.

June is 70. She was a school librarian for 31 years.
We've been married 47 years.
And we both wear dentures. Full uppers and lowers, both of us.
Mine since I was 58. Hers since she was 56.

I want you to know we did it right.
Out at night, every night, no exceptions.
Two cups on the double sink, a Polident tablet in each. Soak till morning. Brush. Rinse. In.
Fourteen years of that for June. Fourteen for me.
I have every box receipt.

Here's what I didn't write down, because I didn't know it was a cost.

June had started clearing her throat after her coffee.
A little "hm-hm" after every sip. I thought it was a habit.
She'd started cutting her food into tiny pieces. She gave up steak.
She said the dentures made it hard. I believed her.

She stopped singing with the Mesa community chorus last fall.
Twelve years, every Tuesday night, alto section.
She said she got winded on the long notes.

And Friday supper at the Mexican place on Main Street, our booth by the window since 1998.
We went twice all winter.
"I'm just not hungry, Hal."

Write those down. They come back.

Tuesday, March 3rd. Pork chops, green beans, a glass of iced tea.
June took a sip of tea with a bite still in her mouth.
And it went down wrong.
She coughed so hard her face went dark red.
She held up one finger. I stood there like a fool with my napkin in my hand.
It lasted maybe a minute.
She laughed it off. "Wrong pipe."
We finished dinner.

Thursday night she had a fever. 101.8.
Friday morning she was breathing fast and shallow, and she couldn't finish a sentence.
I didn't ask her twice. We went in.

The ER doctor showed me her chest X-ray. A white patch, low on the right lung.
Pneumonia.
Then he used a word I'd never heard.
Aspiration.

They admitted her. Six days.
IV antibiotics. Oxygen.
I slept in the vinyl chair by the window.

On day four a young woman came in with a tray.
She said she was the speech-language pathologist.
I said, "June talks fine."
She smiled. "I'm not here for the talking. I'm here for the swallowing."

She called it a swallow test.
Ice chips first. Then a spoon of applesauce. Then a sip of water. Then a cracker.
She rested two fingers on June's throat to feel each swallow.
After the water, June coughed. Just a small one.
The young woman nodded and wrote something down.

Then she explained it. I took notes. I always take notes.

When you swallow, a little flap closes over your windpipe, so food and drink go down to the stomach, not into the lungs.
It's timing. Fractions of a second.
As we get older, the timing slips. The muscles get a little slower.
So sometimes a little food, a little drink, a little saliva goes the wrong way.
That's what aspiration means. Something going down the wrong pipe, into the lungs.
It happens most when you're asleep, she said, when your swallow and your cough are at their weakest.
Young lungs cough it back out. Older lungs don't always.
Aspiration is one of the leading causes of pneumonia in people our age.

"So it was the pork chop," I said.
"Maybe a little," she said. "But it's usually not the food that makes you sick.
It's what rides down with it."

Then she asked to see June's dentures.

She held the upper one up to the window.
"When were these last really cleaned?"
"Every night," I said. "Polident. Fourteen years. I have the receipts."
She tilted the plate toward me.
On the inside, where it sits against the roof of the mouth, there was a dull film. Like a fogged window.
"That," she said. "That's a whole colony.
Every time she eats, drinks or swallows her own spit, a little of it goes down with everything else.
If a little of everything else goes the wrong way, that goes with it."


She said dentures are acrylic, and acrylic is full of tiny pores and scratches you can't see.
The film lives down in there.
A tablet fizzes on the surface. It freshens them. It doesn't get into the pores.
A brush scrubs the surface too. And every scrub carves new scratches.
More scratches, more places for the film to live.
So every morning June put a fresh colony back in her mouth, and every swallow carried a little down.

Back in 1999, in Japanese nursing homes, researchers gave one group of residents daily oral care.
Teeth and dentures, cleaned every day.
The other group got their usual care.
Over two years, about 19 percent of the usual-care group got pneumonia.
In the daily-care group it was about 11 percent.
Fewer of them died of it, too.

I'm an accountant. I know what 19 versus 11 means.
That's not noise. That's a line item.

Then she said the thing I wrote in capital letters on my notepad.
"You can't stop it raining. But you can clean the roof."

I've cleaned our gutters before monsoon season for 30 years. I knew exactly what she meant.
When it rains, whatever is on the roof washes into the gutter and down the spout.
You can't stop the rain. You can't stop swallowing.
But you get to decide what's sitting on the roof.

June's swallow will be a little slower from here on. That's age.
What rides down with it is the part we control.

That night I went home for clean clothes and did something she'd suggested.
I took my own upper plate out of the Polident cup, where it had soaked all night.
I dropped it in a clear drinking glass of warm water and held it up to the kitchen window.
Within a minute there was a faint haze in the water. Little specks drifting off it.
From a denture that had just "soaked" for eight hours.

Try that tomorrow morning. A clear glass. Warm water. Hold it to the window.
Whatever drifts off is what you were about to put back in your mouth.

Then I got the shoeboxes out of the hall closet.

Polident tablets, two a day, fourteen years: $1,084.60.
Adhesive for June, about a tube every three weeks: $1,872.30.
Denture brushes, cups, the travel case she lost in Branson: $214.15.
One ultrasonic cleaner from Amazon, 2019: $31.99.
It died in seven weeks. The receipt outlasted the machine.

Total: $3,203.04.
For dentures that were never actually clean.

And then I took the one from the hospital, and put it on top.
Our share of June's six days was the Medicare Part A deductible. $1,736.
Five thousand dollars, give or take, to learn what a young woman with applesauce told me for free.

So I graded the options, like I would for a client.

Brushing. Cheap. Adds scratches. Fails.
Tablets. Forever money. Works on the surface. Fails.
The $30 pod. Cheap the first time. Then again. And again. Fails.

I called our dentist's office. The hygienist said they clean appliances in an ultrasonic bath.
Ultrasonic cleaning sends sound waves through plain water, tens of thousands of times a second.
Millions of tiny bubbles collapse down into the pores and scratches, where a brush and a tablet can't reach.
The good ones add UV-C light, the kind used to kill germs, so whatever comes loose doesn't just float around.

Then I wrote my criteria on a yellow pad. Five lines. Old habits.

One. Real ultrasonic power. Into the pores. Not a buzzing cup.
Two. Real UV-C light, medical-grade. Kill what comes off.
Three. Tap water only. No tablets, no chemicals. Zero recurring cost.
Four. Five minutes or less. We're both 70-something. If it's slow, we'll skip it.
Five. Built to last, with a real guarantee. Not seven weeks and a dead light.

One came out clean on all five.

DentalPULSE.

I was skeptical. I've audited enough companies to distrust a good-looking claim.
So I read the fine print first.
They ran a 90-day side-by-side lab test of seven leading ultrasonic cleaners.
6 of the 7 left live bacteria behind. DentalPULSE was number one.
42,000 Hz ultrasonic cleaning, medical-grade UV-C light, a stainless steel basin, one button.
It eliminates 99.9% of bacteria in one 5-minute cycle. With tap water.
Dentist-recommended. Rated 4.8 out of 5.
And a 120-day money-back guarantee. That's four months to audit it myself.

Then the math. That's the part I trust.
It was 40% off. $99.95. With a free travel bag and a free dental case.
We'd use it twice a day between us, every day.
That's 730 cycles in the first year. About 14 cents a cycle.
Year two, zero. No tablets to buy. Ever.
Fourteen years of Polident cost us more than ten of these.

I ordered one. One. For the two of us.

June came home on March 9th.
The Pod was waiting on the bathroom counter, right between our two old cups.

That first night, I ran mine first. I wanted to see it fail.
Tap water. Teeth in. One button. Five minutes.
When it finished, the water was cloudy. Gray. Little flecks floating on top.
June leaned over my shoulder and said, "Hal. That came off yours."
"That came off mine."
Then she ran hers. Same thing. Maybe worse, and she would want me to say maybe.

Here's our routine now.
At night, his, five minutes. Then hers, five minutes. Ten minutes for both of us, start to finish.
Then into the cases for the night.
The two cups went in the garage sale. The Polident went with them.

I'll be honest about week one. Nothing dramatic happened.
June was still tired. She was getting over pneumonia.
That takes time, and the antibiotics did the real work.
I can't tell you a cleaner would have kept June out of that hospital.
I'm an accountant. I don't book what I can't prove.
What I can tell you is what's on her dentures now when she swallows.
And it isn't that fog on the inside of the plate.

She does her chin-tuck swallow, the way the speech pathologist taught her.
Small bites. Small sips. Sitting up straight at meals.
And clean teeth, every single day.

At her follow-up, eight weeks later, they checked her swallow again.
The speech pathologist looked in her mouth and at the dentures.
She said, "These look really good. Whatever you two are doing, keep doing it."
June pointed at me. "He keeps the books."

Now let me close out the ledger. I promised you every line.

In May, June went back to the chorus. Tuesday night, alto section.
She told me the long notes came back the third week.
I sat in the folding chairs at the spring concert and didn't hear a single cough.

The first Friday in April, we went back to the Mexican place on Main Street.
The booth by the window was open.
June ordered the carne asada. Cut small. Chin tuck. Every bite.
She finished it.

And the coffee. That little "hm-hm" after every sip.
It's mostly gone now. Not all the way. She's 70. But mostly.
I notice every morning. I count.

Here's the last thing.
The shoebox for this year is on the shelf in the hall closet.
The receipt from the hospital is still in there. I'll never throw it out.
But it's not the one on top anymore.
On top is a receipt for $99.95.
One cleaner. Two sets of teeth.
It's the best money I've spent since the toaster.
