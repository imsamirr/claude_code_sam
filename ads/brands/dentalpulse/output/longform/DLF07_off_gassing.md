## PRIMARY TEXT

My granddaughter leaned away from me in the middle of a bedtime story.
Not a lot.
Just far enough that I noticed.
She's six. Six-year-olds don't lean away to be polite.

Then she asked me, in her honest little voice,
"Grandma, why does your mouth smell like the basement?"

I finished the page.
I don't remember a word of it.

It wasn't my breath.
I know that now.
It took a hygienist from my church choir about four minutes to explain what it really was.
It's a word I'd only ever heard about new carpet.
Off-gassing.

Let me back up.

I'm Gail. I'm 66. I live in Rapid City, South Dakota.
I answered phones at a veterinary clinic for 24 years. I talk. It's what I do.
I've worn full dentures, upper and lower, for nine years.

And every Friday night, my granddaughter Lily sleeps over.
Friday is story night. It has been since she was two.
Lily picks the book. I do the voices.
The owl is my best one. She makes me do it twice.

So you understand what that question did to me.

I did everything right. I want you to know that.
Every night, my dentures went in a cup with a Polident tablet. Blue water, minty in the morning.
I brushed them twice a day with denture paste.
Mouthwash three times a day, a $7 bottle a week.
Sugar-free mints in every coat pocket. A tongue scraper. A breath spray.
I asked my denturist if they were trapping food. He did a reline. $240.
"Your mouth looks healthy," he said. "Dentures just have a bit of an odor sometimes. Soak them longer."

So I soaked them longer.
Close to $400 a year, to smell like mint.
And under the mint, the basement.

Here's what I'd already given up without telling anyone.

I sing alto in our church choir. For six years I stood in the front row.
Last spring I moved to the back and mouthed the big notes. I'd noticed people turning their heads.

I stopped kissing Lily on the cheek goodnight.
I kissed the top of her head instead. I told myself it was the same.

And Friday nights, I'd started sitting at the foot of her bed instead of up next to her.
"So I can see the pictures better," I said.
That's how far away I was when she leaned.

That night, after she fell asleep, I went into my bathroom and shut the door.
11:40 p.m.
I took my dentures out and smelled them. Mint. Nothing else.
I put them back in, looked in the mirror and read the back of the toothpaste tube out loud.
There it was.
Only when I talked.

I sat on the edge of the tub.
Nine years. Who else had leaned away and been too polite to say so?

That Wednesday, at choir practice, I stood in the back row with my hand over my mouth.
Afterward, at the coffee table in the fellowship hall, Rhonda came over.
Rhonda sings first soprano. She's been a dental hygienist for 30 years.
She looked at my hand and said,
"Can I ask you a hygienist question?"

I said yes.
"Is it worse when you talk? And can you not smell it when you take them out?"

I almost dropped my coffee.

"It's not your breath, Gail. It's the denture."

Denture acrylic looks solid. It's not.
It's full of tiny pores, too small to see.
And it drinks. Acrylic soaks up about 2 to 3 percent of its own weight in water. And whatever is dissolved in that water goes in with it.

Under a denture, where it's warm and dark and there's not much air, bacteria live.
Those bacteria make sulfur gases. The rotten-egg smell.
The acrylic soaks those up too. All day and all night.

Then you put them in, and your mouth warms them up.
You start to talk. Warm air moves over them.
And they breathe it back out.

"Off-gassing," Rhonda said. "Same as a new carpet. Except this isn't glue. It's what the bacteria made."

That's why I couldn't smell them in my hand.
That's why it came when I talked. And when I sang.

Then she gave me a picture I can't forget.
"You know a kitchen sponge that's gone sour?"
I did. Everyone does.
"You can rinse it. Squeeze it. Squirt lemon dish soap on it. It smells lemony for an hour. Then sour again. Because the smell isn't on the sponge. It's in it."

The mints were air freshener sprayed on the sponge.
The mouthwash was rinsing the sponge.
The tablet was soaking the sponge in minty water overnight. Fresh in the morning. Sour by lunch.
And brushing hard was scrubbing a sponge with a wire brush. More holes. More room.

"The tablet works on the surface," Rhonda said. "Your problem lives inside."

Then her face changed.
"Gail, the smell is the part you notice. It's not the part I worry about."

That same film, she said, is where yeast lives. Candida.
It causes something called denture stomatitis. The roof of your mouth under the denture goes red and sore.
Studies have found it in up to about two-thirds of people who wear dentures.
And you can treat the mouth all you want. If the yeast is still living in the denture, it goes right back in.

Then she told me about a study that kept me up that night.
In 2016, the Journal of Dental Research published a study of older patients in the hospital.
In most of them, about 64 percent, their dentures were carrying germs that can cause pneumonia.
Germs riding on the thing you put in your mouth every morning.
Germs you can breathe in when you're asleep or sick, when your body is slower to cough them out.

"Then what gets it out of the sponge?" I asked.
"You don't scrub a sponge from the outside," she said. "You squeeze it from the inside."

At her office, every appliance goes in an ultrasonic bath.
Ultrasonic cleaning sends sound waves through water tens of thousands of times a second. Tiny bubbles form and collapse right down inside the pores. Squeezing the sponge from the inside.
Then UV-C light, the germ-killing kind, for whatever comes loose.

She wrote what to look for on the back of my church bulletin.

Real ultrasonic. About 42,000 pulses a second. Not a vibrating cup.
Medical-grade UV-C. Not a blue night-light.
Tap water only. "Nothing minty," she said. "You want to smell the truth."
Stainless steel inside.
Quick, or you won't do it every day.

Then she gave me a test to do that night.
Take your dentures out. Rinse them under the tap.
Put them in a clean glass jar with a lid. Close it. Wait ten minutes.
Open it, put your nose right at the rim and breathe in.

I did it at 10 p.m. They'd had a minty soak the night before.
I won't describe it.

Then I looked at the roof of my mouth with a flashlight. Pinker than I'd like.
My dentist saw me that week. A little irritation under the upper, she said. Early.
I'm not a doctor. I won't tell you what that film would have done to me in ten years.
I'll tell you I didn't want to find out with Lily on my lap.

My son-in-law had a $30 one from Amazon. It hummed. No light. I ran mine in it twice and did the jar test. Same basement.

So I went looking, and I found a 90-day side-by-side lab test of 7 leading ultrasonic cleaners.
6 of the 7 left live bacteria behind.
One came out #1.

DentalPULSE.

I sat at my kitchen table and went down Rhonda's list.

Stainless steel basin. Check.

And it eliminates 99.9% of bacteria in that one cycle.

I was still nervous. I'd already spent a fortune on things that smelled nice and fixed nothing. And I'd just watched a $30 one hum and do nothing.
What tipped me was this. It uses only tap water. There's nothing minty in it to fool my nose. If it didn't work, the jar would tell me.
And it came with a 120-day money-back guarantee.
That's a lot of jar tests.
And it was 40% off, $99.95.
Less than what I was spending every three months on mouthwash and mints.

It came on a Monday.
Tap water in the steel basin. Both dentures in. One button.
Five minutes.
The water went cloudy.
Little flecks sat on the bottom of the steel.
These were dentures I'd soaked the night before and brushed that morning.
I stood there in my robe and thought about the sponge.

Then I did the jar test.
Wet. Plastic. That's all.
I did it again an hour later because I didn't believe it.
Nine years of minty soaks, and the first clean jar came from tap water.

I'll be honest about week one.
It wasn't magic.
On day three I caught it once, singing in the car.
I called Rhonda. She laughed.
"Nine years in the sponge, Gail. Give it a few weeks of squeezing."

So I ran it every morning while my coffee brewed.
By the end of week two, the jar test came back clean every single time.
I threw out the mouthwash. I stopped buying mints.
I found a tin in my winter coat in November and gave it to the mailman.

Week three, I moved back to the front row of the altos.
I sang the big notes.
Rhonda turned around from the sopranos and gave me a thumbs-up during the last hymn.

My dentist looked at the roof of my mouth again in November.
"That's calmed right down. Whatever you're doing, keep doing it."

And Friday.
Lily picked a book about a lost owl. Of course she did.
I didn't sit at the foot of the bed.
I climbed right in next to her.
I did the owl voice. She made me do it again.
Halfway through the second time, she put her head on my shoulder.
She leaned in.
She fell asleep there, breathing on my sweater.

I kissed her on the cheek.

My daughter Megan picked her up Saturday morning. Lily ran to the car shouting that Grandma did the owl twice.
Megan looked at me over the car roof.
"You're back up by the pillow?"
I didn't know she'd noticed I'd ever left.
