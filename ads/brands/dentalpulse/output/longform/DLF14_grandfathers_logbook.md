## PRIMARY TEXT

My late grandfather ran a commercial dental lab on Pratt Street in Hartford for decades.

I never touched his old workbench notebook until my wife Helen ended up in the emergency room with a lung infection.

Under the bench in his garage workshop was his canvas logbook.

Last November, the weather turned cold in Connecticut.

Helen developed a wet, rattling cough.

I drove her straight to the emergency room at the regional hospital.

The doctors found a bacterial lung infection.

They gave her heavy antibiotics.

They put her on oxygen.

They kept her in the hospital for four days.

Four months later, in early spring, the rattle came back in her chest.

Another fever hit.

Another round of antibiotics.

Her doctor sat down in the clinic.

He asked a question that caught me off guard:

"Does Helen wear dentures, and does she ever sleep with them in?"

I asked what false teeth had to do with her lungs.

The doctor explained aspiration.

When we sleep, our swallowing reflex slows down.

Tiny droplets of saliva carry mouth germs past the vocal cords.

Those germs slide straight down into the lungs.

The doctor showed me the medical research.

In 2015, a study by Iinuma in the Journal of Dental Research tracked 524 seniors for three years.

Seniors who slept in their dentures had more than twice the pneumonia risk.

They also had much higher levels of denture plaque and Candida yeast.

In 2016, a study by O'Donnell in the Journal of Prosthodontics tested elderly hospital patients.

Germs that cause pneumonia were found on the dentures of about 64% of those tested.

Back in 1999, a study in The Lancet by Yoneyama looked at Japanese nursing homes.

Daily mouth care cut pneumonia from 19% down to 11%.

It also cut pneumonia deaths.

In 2014, a study by Quinn in the Journal of Nursing Scholarship tested hospital patients.

Daily oral cleaning cut pneumonia by about 37%.

"A denture plate is porous acrylic," the doctor told me.

"Fizzy tablets leave live bacterial biofilm and yeast deep inside the plastic.

Every breath pulls those germs straight into her lungs."

That night, while Helen rested, I walked out to my garage workshop where I keep my grandfather Edward's old oak tool chest.

My grandfather had run a commercial dental lab on Pratt Street in Hartford for decades.

He made dentures, partials, and bridges for dentists across Connecticut.

In the bottom drawer was his workbench notebook.

I sat under the shop light and turned the pages.

On page 28, I saw what he wrote in black fountain-pen ink:

"Never trust fizzing tablets to clean processed acrylic.

Tablets are cosmetic bleach.

They release peroxide and soap that bubble on the surface and hide odor.

They cannot penetrate biofilm.

Acrylic is porous like bone.

It absorbs saliva, bacteria, and yeast into tiny cavities.

Commercial labs clean appliances only in ultrasonic baths.

Sound waves create tiny vacuum bubbles that collapse against the plastic.

That strips biofilm out of the pores without scratching the surface."

What I saw made my skin crawl.

The stiff bristles from her brush had carved thousands of tiny scratches across the pink plastic.
