He asked like a prosecutor trying to catch a lying witness on cross-examination.

Dr. Vance had the metal pick pressed right against the biting groove of my upper left second molar.

He tapped it once.

He tapped it again.

Then he sighed, pulled off his magnifying loupes, and looked down at me over his glasses.

"Carmen, be honest with me," he said. "How much soda or sweet tea are you sipping during your court days?"

"None," I said, my mouth dry from the cotton rolls. "I drink black coffee at six in the morning and plain tap water the rest of the day. I have not bought a soda since college."

He did not believe me.

He wrote on my chart and looked back at me with that stern, disappointed look doctors give you.

"Are you sure?" he asked. "Because you have three brand-new, deep cavities on your back molars. Rapid acid demineralization. I usually only see this in teenagers who drink two liters of Coke every day."

I felt my cheeks flush red with humiliation.

I am thirty-eight years old.

I work as a certified federal court interpreter, driving down I-10 to the courthouse in El Paso every morning.

My entire career is built on accuracy, credibility, and neutral facts.

Being questioned like a child sneaking candy made my stomach turn.

The truth was, I took obsessive care of my teeth.

I brushed twice a day with an electric toothbrush.

I flossed every evening without fail.

I never missed my cleanings, and in my entire twenties, I had never had a single cavity.

The only dental issue I had started four years ago.

Under the stress of interpreting murder trials and border depositions, I developed severe bruxism.

I was clenching and grinding my teeth in my sleep.

My jaw clicked, my neck was stiff, and my front teeth showed micro-cracks along the edges.

So Dr. Vance made me a custom hard acrylic night guard.

It cost $550 out of pocket.

It stopped the grinding and saved my jaw.

Every morning for four years, I followed the printed care sheet:

Over the years, the clear plastic had turned cloudy.

A chalky white film had settled into the deep molar grooves, along with faint yellow stains where my teeth bit down.

I assumed that was normal wear on plastic.

And now, here I was, facing three composite fillings, drilling into healthy enamel, and over $600 out of pocket after insurance.

Dr. Vance stepped out to review an emergency x-ray.

Andrea, the hygienist who had cleaned my teeth for eight years, stepped up to polish.

I was blinking back tears of frustration.

"Andrea, I swear to you I do not drink soda. I do not know what is happening to me. Maybe my enamel is giving out now that I am approaching forty."

"Did you bring it with you today?"

"It is in my purse."

I handed her the little plastic case.

The smooth front edges washed clean.

But right in the deep biting troughs of the molars, the acrylic turned a violent, electric purple.

Molar #14.

Molar #15.

Lower molar #19.

The exact three teeth where Dr. Vance had just diagnosed three deep cavities.

I stared at the bright purple stains.

"Wait, what does that even mean?" I asked.

Andrea held the guard up against the overhead light.

"It means you do not have a soda habit, Carmen," she said. "You have a dirty plastic habit. This night guard gave you those cavities."

I could not wrap my head around it.

"Think about what a night guard actually does," Andrea explained. "It is a sealed plastic lid. You clamp it tightly over your teeth for eight hours every single night."

"When you are awake, saliva constantly washes over your enamel, neutralizing acid and protecting your teeth."

"When you sleep, saliva production drops by over seventy percent. And your night guard acts as an airtight wall that blocks whatever saliva you have from touching your teeth."

"Now look at what you have been doing to clean it. You have been scrubbing it with a toothbrush and toothpaste."

"Toothpaste contains abrasive silica designed to polish hard mineral enamel. But dental acrylic is soft plastic. Every time you brushed this guard, you carved millions of microscopic gouges and scratches into the material."

"Rinsing with tap water does nothing to biofilm. Blue soak tablets only bubble on the slick surface. Deep down inside those scratches, the bacteria thrive."
