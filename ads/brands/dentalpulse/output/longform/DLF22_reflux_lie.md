## PRIMARY TEXT

For two years, I kept a roll of antacids hidden behind the flour bags in our bakery.

Every morning at four o'clock, my husband Stan walked into the kitchen with black coffee.

As soon as his back was turned to check the ovens, I popped two chalky tablets into my mouth and chewed them as fast as I could.

Whenever he caught me, I gave him the same tired excuse.

"Just my acid reflux acting up again, honey," I would say. "Too much coffee on an empty stomach."

Stan believed me, worried about me, and bought bottles of antacids to keep by the sink while having no idea I was lying to his face every single day.

The burning in my mouth was not coming from my stomach.

My stomach was fine, but the fire was on the roof of my mouth. Right across my upper palate, right where my full acrylic denture clicked into place against my gums.

It felt like someone had pressed a scalding hot spoon against the skin behind my front teeth and held it there. It was a raw, stinging ache that made every breath feel like hot sandpaper.

And the reason I lied to my husband was pure shame.

I am sixty-six years old. Stan and I have run our small bakery in Lancaster for twenty-eight years. People come from three towns over for our Dutch crumb cake, our sourdough loaves, and our scratch fruit pies.

In this town, I am Donna the baker, not the woman whose mouth is rotting under false teeth.

Four years ago, after three failed root canals and bone loss, my dentist pulled my upper teeth. I got a full upper denture. Stan was loving, but inside, I felt like I had aged thirty years overnight.

I became obsessed with keeping them clean.

Every night, I soaked them in blue bubbling Polident tablets. Every morning, I scrubbed them with a stiff brush until the acrylic squeaked.

And yet, two years ago, the burning started.

At first, it was just a dull tingle with hot tea. But within months, it felt like an open flame.

The worst part was what it did to my baking.

When the roof of your mouth is inflamed and raw, your taste buds shut down. Sweet things taste sour. Butter tastes greasy. Delicate spices disappear.

I stopped tasting my own pie fillings, stirring the lemon curd and handing a wooden spoon to a counter girl to ask if the sugar was right.

Then came a Tuesday morning in early spring that broke my heart.

George Miller, a retired postal carrier who has bought crumb cake from us every Tuesday for twenty years, brought a box back to the counter.

He looked uncomfortable.

"Donna, I hate to say anything," he muttered, looking down at his boots. "But this morning's crumb cake tasted off. Did you change the vanilla or forget the salt?"

I stood behind the counter, apologized, handed him fresh sourdough, and refused his money.

As soon as he walked out, I ran to the back pantry and cried into my apron.

Stan walked in and found me wiping my face.

He saw the empty roll of antacids on the table.

"That is it," Stan said. "You have been chewing those tablets for two years, Donna. You are in pain every morning, and now it is hurting your work. I am calling Dr. Albright today."

Dr. Albright was a gastroenterologist across town. Stan booked an appointment for Thursday.

Stan drove us there. He held my hand in the waiting room. He walked into the exam room and spoke for me because he thought he was protecting his wife.

"Doctor, she has had severe acid reflux for two years," Stan told him. "Every morning around four, her mouth and throat burn like fire. She goes through a bottle of antacids every two weeks."

Dr. Albright nodded, pulled on gloves, clicked on a silver penlight, and picked up a tongue depressor.

"All right, Donna," he said. "Let us take a look. Open wide for me."

I opened my mouth.

Dr. Albright looked for maybe four seconds.

Then he stopped. He lowered the tongue depressor and looked at me with a puzzled expression.

"Donna," he asked. "Do you wear a full upper denture?"

My face went hot with shame. I glanced at Stan, who looked confused.

"Yes," I whispered.

"Take it out for me, please," Dr. Albright said gently.

With trembling fingers, I reached in, broke the suction, and lifted out my upper plate. I wrapped it quickly in a tissue.

Dr. Albright picked up a small mirror, angled his light, and turned the mirror toward Stan.

"Stan, look in here," the doctor said.

Stan leaned in. His eyes went wide.

The entire roof of my mouth was bright, fiery red. It looked like an angry sheet of raw meat, swollen with cobblestone bumps, shaped in the exact outline of my acrylic plate.

"Stan, your wife does not have acid reflux," Dr. Albright said. "Her stomach is fine."

He looked at me with kind eyes.

"Donna, you have a classic case of severe denture stomatitis," he said. "It is an aggressive Candida yeast infection that colonizes the tissue under dental appliances."

I sat on the exam table clutching my tissue.

"A yeast infection?" I asked. "Wait, what does that even mean? I soak them in fizzy tablets every night! I scrub them with a brush!"

Dr. Albright shook his head.

"Donna, denture stomatitis affects up to two-thirds of denture wearers," he explained. "It has nothing to do with being dirty. But fizzy tablets and brushes will never fix it."

He explained that dental acrylic is porous resin full of microscopic pits.

Candida yeast burrows deep inside those tiny pores, feeding on moisture. Over time, it weaves into a dense, sticky fungal biofilm.

"Antacids do nothing to yeast," he said. "And drugstore soaking tablets only fizz on the surface. They cannot pull yeast out of microscopic pores."

Worse, he explained that stiff brushing cuts microscopic scratches into the acrylic, giving yeast and bacteria more places to hide while locking in stubborn yellow stains and cloudy white film.

"Every morning at four o'clock, you snap that porous plate back in," Dr. Albright told me. "The plate instantly re-seeds the Candida yeast right back into your palate. You have been caught in a continuous cycle of reinfection for two years."

My denture plate caused the burning.

Stan reached over and squeezed my hand. He was not disgusted. He was just relieved to know the truth.

Dr. Albright sent me down the hall to a prosthodontist who specializes in dentures.

The prosthodontist was direct. First, he prescribed Nystatin, an antifungal mouth rinse with a forty-five-dollar copay. I swished it four times a day. For three days, the burning calmed down.

But on day four, the minute I wore my plate through a shift at the bakery, the sting came roaring back. The rinse could soothe my gums, but it could not sterilize porous acrylic. The plate re-seeded the infection immediately.

I tried scrubbing with a new nine-dollar paste. The yellow stains stayed stuck, and the white film came right back.

Then Stan ordered a thirty-dollar ultrasonic cleaner from an ad. It rattled across the counter, and in two weeks the motor died. It had no UV light, and the plate still came out slimy.

I went back to the prosthodontist in tears.

"Am I going to have to throw this four-thousand-dollar denture away?" I asked.

The prosthodontist smiled and shook his head.

"No, Donna," he said. "You do not need new teeth. You just need clinical-grade cleaning technology. In clinics, we use 42,000 Hz ultrasonic sound waves and medical-grade ultraviolet light. You need that same technology at home."
