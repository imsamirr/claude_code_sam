## PRIMARY TEXT

I paid $5,800 for straight teeth.
I got them.
I also got two fillings, and a tooth my dentist is watching for a root canal.

Eighteen months of plastic in my mouth.
Twenty-two hours a day.
I did every single thing the little card told me to do.

At my final appointment, the hygienist held my tray up to the window.
Then she said four words I still hear every morning.
"Priya. This is a lid."

I'm writing this because nobody said those words to me on day one.
And because three weeks ago my sister got married, and I almost didn't smile in a single picture.
If you have plastic on your teeth right now, please read to the end.
You're going to want to check something tonight.

Let me back up.

My name is Priya.
I'm 34.
I run payroll for a trucking company outside Raleigh, North Carolina.
I count other people's money all day.
So believe me, I counted this.

$5,800 for Invisalign.
$1,900 of it went on a credit card I'm still paying off.

I had one front tooth that sat on top of the one next to it.
In sixth grade a boy called it my fang.
I've smiled with my mouth closed in every photo since.
My college graduation.
My cousin's wedding.
My work badge, which I look at every single day.

Then my little sister Anjali got engaged.
She asked me to be her maid of honor.
That same night I booked a consult.
I wanted one thing.
To stand next to her at her wedding and smile like a normal person.

So I was the perfect patient.
I wore my trays 22 hours a day.
I set alarms on my phone.
I took them out to eat.

And I cleaned my trays exactly the way I was told.

Here is what "clean" meant.

Every morning, I brushed my trays with my toothbrush and a little toothpaste.
That's what the front desk told me when I asked.
"Just brush them like your teeth."

At work, I rinsed them under the bathroom tap and snapped them back in.

When they were out, they lived in the little plastic case in my purse.
Closed.
Dark.
Damp.
All day.

And every afternoon at 3, my work friend Marisol and I walked to the coffee place on the corner.
Iced coffee.
Oat milk.
Two pumps of vanilla.
I drank it through a straw with my trays in, because a video online said a straw was fine.
Those 15 minutes were the best part of my day.

I thought I was being careful.
I was being careful.
That's the part that still makes me angry.

Around month six, my trays got cloudy.
Not dirty.
Foggy, like a shower door.

Then the case started to smell when I opened it.
Sour.
Like the inside of an old lunchbox.

At month eleven, a lower back tooth zinged when I drank cold water.
"Your teeth are moving. Some sensitivity is totally normal."
Totally normal.

My final check was on a Tuesday in March.
I took a selfie in the car before I went in.
Big smile.
Teeth showing.
Trays in my hand like a trophy.

Forty minutes later I was looking at my own X-ray.
Two cavities between my back teeth.
And a third spot on a lower molar.
The one that zinged.

She said the deep one was close to the nerve.
"We'll fill it and watch it. If it starts to throb at night, call us. That would be a root canal."

The fillings were $460 after insurance.
A root canal and a crown, if it comes to that, is about $2,400.
On top of the $5,800.

I sat in my car in the parking deck for a long time.
I opened the selfie.
I looked at the straight teeth I had paid for.
And I thought, what did I do wrong?

I'll tell you what I did wrong.
Nothing anyone ever told me was wrong.

Here is the part I need you to hear.

Before I left, the hygienist came out to the hall.
Her name is Gloria. Reading glasses on a beaded chain, 26 years of cleaning teeth.

She asked me a question nobody had asked in eighteen months.
"How do you clean your trays?"

I told her.
The toothpaste.
The tap.
The case in my purse.
The 3 o'clock coffee.

She held my tray up to the window.
In the light I could see it.
Thin scratches all over the inside.
And a white film packed into every groove where my teeth sit.

"Priya. This is a lid."

Then she explained it in a way I will never forget.

Your teeth have a security guard, she said.
Spit.
All day long, saliva washes your teeth and calms down the acid that germs make.

But when the tray is in, saliva can't get in there.
Whatever is on the inside of this tray sits right on your enamel.
With no guard.
Up to 22 hours a day.

"The coffee," she said.
"You made a cup out of plastic, poured sweet coffee into it, and pressed it against your teeth every afternoon."

Then the toothpaste.
Toothpaste is gritty on purpose, she said.
It's made to polish hard enamel.
On soft plastic, it scratches.
Every scratch is a tiny ditch.
The film settles down in the ditches, and your brush rides right over the top.
That's why it goes cloudy.

The tap water takes off what's loose.
The film isn't loose.
It's glued on.

And the case in my purse?
"Warm, dark and damp all day. You know what grows like that."

Then she said the thing I think about most.

"You know when you leave leftovers in a container in the back of the fridge?
A lid doesn't keep things clean.
It keeps things in."

Then she squeezed my arm.
"You did what you were told. Nobody told you the right thing."

Here is what made it worse.
I'm not done with plastic.
Now I wear clear retainers every night.
For the rest of my life, if I want to keep these teeth straight.
Same plastic.
Same lid.
Every night.

The film has a name.
Biofilm.
It's a layer of germs that glues itself to a surface and builds a little shelter over itself.
It hides in tiny pores and scratches in plastic.
A brush can't reach down into a scratch.
And every time you scrub, you make more.

I'd been building my own germ hotel every morning with my toothbrush.

I also tried the fizzy cleaning crystals.
$28 a month.
They fizzed.
The tray smelled minty for an hour.
The film was still there when I ran my nail inside it.
Fizz works on the surface.
The problem lives in the scratches.

I texted Gloria a week later.
I asked her one question.
"What do you use at the office? You don't scrub your tools with a toothbrush."

She wrote back:
"Nope. Ultrasonic bath. Then we sterilize."

Ultrasonic.

Sound waves move through water and make millions of tiny bubbles.
The bubbles pop right against the surface.
They reach down into the scratches and grooves a brush skates over.
Nothing touches the plastic, so nothing new gets scratched.

Then there's UV-C light.
The kind of light used to kill germs on surfaces.

So I made a list, like I do with everything.
My trays and retainers needed five things.

One. Nothing touches it. No brush, no paste, no new scratches.
Two. It reaches inside the grooves. Real ultrasonic power, not a buzz.
Three. It kills what's left. Medical-grade UV-C, not a pretty blue bulb.
Four. Just water. No chemicals on plastic that sits on my teeth all night.
Five. Fast. If it takes longer than my coffee, I'll quit by week two.

Gloria had one more warning.
"Don't buy the $30 one from Amazon."

She was right.
I found a 90-day lab test of seven popular ultrasonic cleaners.
Six of the seven left live bacteria behind.
Six of seven.

One passed all five of my things.

DentalPULSE.

It's a white pod with one button.
42,000 Hz ultrasonic cleaning, plus medical-grade UV-C light.
One 5-minute cycle.
Tap water only.
No tablets, no chemicals, no subscription.
A stainless steel basin.
It eliminates 99.9% of bacteria in one cycle.

It was $99.95.
I had just paid $460 for fillings.
I might pay $2,400 for a root canal.
I'd paid $5,800 for the teeth.
I almost laughed.

I'll be honest.
I was skeptical.
I've been sold a lot of things online, and half of them are in a drawer.

The first time I ran it, I put in the retainer I had cleaned that morning.
With a soft brush.
No toothpaste.
The way I'd been doing it since March.

Five minutes later the water was cloudy.
Little white flecks were sitting at the bottom of the steel basin.
From a retainer I had already cleaned.

I stood there in my robe and just stared at it.

Week one, I didn't feel anything different.
I want to be honest about that.
It's not magic.
It's a habit.
Retainer out, Pod on, brush my teeth, coffee on.
By the time the coffee is ready, it's done.

Week two, I did the fingernail test Gloria showed me.
I ran my nail along the inside of a front tooth on the retainer.
Nothing on my nail.
Nothing to smell.

Week three, I opened the case and didn't make a face.
That was a first.

Week six, the retainer was still clear.
I know scratches don't come out.
My old aligners were scratched for good.
But my new retainer never got scratched in the first place, because nothing scrubs it anymore.

At my six-month cleaning, Gloria held my retainer up to the same window.
She turned it in the light.
Then she ran her own fingernail inside it and showed me.
Clean.
"Whatever you're doing, keep doing it."

I'm not going to pretend a cleaner fixed a tooth.
Nothing fixes a cavity but a dentist.
The tooth they're watching is still being watched.
So far it hasn't throbbed.

I can't promise you anything about your teeth.
I can tell you what's on my retainer now.
Nothing.

Marisol and I still walk to the corner at 3.
Iced coffee.
Oat milk.
Two pumps of vanilla.
The only lid on anything now is the one on my cup.

I got a new work badge in September.
I smiled with my teeth.
The IT guy said, "Whoa, did you get a haircut?"

And three weeks ago, I stood next to Anjali at her wedding.
Her photographer kept saying, "Bigger, maid of honor, bigger."
So I gave her bigger.
Every picture.
Teeth showing.
My sister cried when she saw the proofs.
So did I.

The first selfie I took that Tuesday in March is still on my phone.
The smile was real.
The teeth were straight.
And under that plastic, I was losing them, and I had no idea.

The photo from the wedding is the one I framed.
