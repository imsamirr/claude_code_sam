## PRIMARY TEXT

My older son went through braces four years ago, and it ended with twenty-two hundred dollars in retreatment bills and crooked teeth.

Last Thursday, my fifteen-year-old daughter walked through our front door holding the exact same plastic retainer case.

I felt a cold knot tighten in my stomach.

My name is Valerie. I am forty-eight years old, and I work as a loan officer at a credit union in Columbia, South Carolina. When you look at numbers on loan applications all day, you get very good at tracking where money leaks out of a household.

In my kitchen desk, I have a green hanging file folder labeled "Orthodontics."

Inside that folder is a paper trail that still makes my blood boil.

When my son Luke was in eighth grade, we paid $4,800 for his braces. Two years of appointments, broken brackets, wax on his gums, and checks written to the orthodontist every single month.

When the braces finally came off, the orthodontist handed Luke a clear plastic retainer and told him to wear it every night.

Six months later, the behavior mystery started.

Luke started "losing" his retainer.

First, he told me he lost it in the locker room after cross-country practice. A replacement retainer cost $400 out of pocket. Insurance does not cover replacements. I wrote the check.

Four months after that, he claimed he left it at a friend's lake house over Labor Day weekend. Another $400 check.

Then, halfway through his junior year, I was cleaning his bedroom. His heavy canvas track bag was zipped up in the corner of his closet. I unzipped the front pocket to pull out his dirty jerseys for the wash.

Down at the bottom, crumpled under an old roll of athletic tape, was a greasy paper napkin from a fast-food drive-through.

I unrolled the napkin.

Luke's four-hundred-dollar retainer was inside.

It was repulsive. The clear plastic had turned a murky, milky yellow. Along the edges and inside the deep molar grooves was a crust of chalky white film. And when the napkin opened, a smell hit me that made me pull my head back.

It smelled like rotten, sour saliva and swamp water.

I took the retainer downstairs, set it on the kitchen island, and waited for Luke to come home from practice.

When he walked in, I pointed at the napkin.

"Why is your four-hundred-dollar retainer buried in your track bag?" I asked him.

He looked at the floor.

"Mom," he said quietly. "I didn't lose the other ones either. I threw them away."

I stared at him.

"You threw away eight hundred dollars of retainers?"

"Because it smells like vomit," he said. His voice cracked. "I brush it with toothpaste every morning. I soak it in those blue Efferdent tablets like you told me to. But after two weeks, it turns yellow and gets this gross white film. When I put it in my mouth before bed, I can taste the sour junk trapped in the plastic. The guys on the team smelled it from across the room at camp. I can't put that gross thing in my mouth."

So Luke stopped wearing it.

By the spring of his senior year, the disaster hit. His two front teeth had twisted, overlapping by nearly two millimeters. The bite had collapsed backward.

Relapse.

Because he had stopped wearing the gross retainer, his teeth had shifted right back. To fix it before college, we had to pay $1,800 for a round of retreatment clear aligners, plus another $400 for a brand-new set of retainers once his teeth were straight again.

Total money spent on Luke's teeth: $7,400.

So last Thursday, when my fifteen-year-old daughter Abby came home from the orthodontist after getting her braces removed, carrying that same teal plastic case, my hands started shaking as I took the box from her.

We had just spent $5,100 on Abby's braces.

Her smile looked gorgeous. But I knew what was coming. Within four weeks, her retainer would turn cloudy, grow yellow stains, start smelling like rotten saliva, and end up wrapped in a napkin in the trash.

That evening, I called my sister-in-law, Darcy.

Darcy has worked as a senior orthodontic assistant at a large dental practice in Greenville for fourteen years. She sees hundreds of teenagers and adults every single month.

I told her the whole story about Luke. The $2,200 retreatment bill. The greasy napkin in the track bag. The gross yellow plastic.

"Darcy," I said. "Why does this happen? Luke scrubbed his retainer with a toothbrush and Crest every day. He soaked it in Polident and Efferdent tablets. Why did it turn yellow and smell like a sewer?"

Darcy sighed on the phone.

"Valerie, nobody explains this to parents," she said. "Retainer plastic looks completely smooth to the human eye. But under a microscope, that clear plastic is actually porous, like a hard kitchen sponge. And your mouth is filled with bacteria and yeast."

She told me to do a quick free test right then.

"Go to Luke's bathroom," Darcy said. "Take his current retainer and hold it right up against a bare light bulb. What do you see?"

I walked into the bathroom and held Luke's retainer up to the vanity bulb.

"It looks cloudy," I said. "Like frosted glass. And there's a chalky white film in the crevices where his molars sit."

"That is bacterial biofilm," Darcy said. "When you brush a retainer with toothpaste, you are actually ruining it. Toothpaste contains abrasive scrubbing particles meant for hard tooth enamel. Plus, the chemicals in those tablets can make the plastic brittle over time. The bacteria stay alive, producing sulfur compounds that smell like rotten eggs. Kids hate the smell and taste, so they stop wearing it. And teenage teeth can shift in as little as three weeks."
