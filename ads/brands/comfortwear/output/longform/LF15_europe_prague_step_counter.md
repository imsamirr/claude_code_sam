## PRIMARY TEXT

I was moving my mom's photos onto her new phone when I saw the step counter from our trip to Prague.

Day one: 16,412 steps.

Day two: 11,950.

Day three: 2,108.

It took a castle she never saw, a fight at a dinner table, and a 77-year-old widow on our tour bus to find out what happened between day two and day three.

Mom never told me. And the worst part is, I bought the shoes.

Let me back up.

My mom, Maureen, is 68. She cooked in a hospital cafeteria for 27 years. Ten-hour days on a slick tile floor, on her feet all shift, and she never once called in sick.

When my dad passed away three years ago, she made a standing rule for herself: no more waiting for someday.

Prague had been on her fridge on a magnet for 30 years. So last April, she and I and my daughter Sophie, 14, went. Ten days. Prague, then a little castle town in the south called Český Krumlov.

I wanted her to have the absolute best of everything, so two weeks before the flight I bought her the shoes everybody online recommended for travel. Hokas. The big, thick ones. A hundred and sixty-five dollars. I wrapped them like a birthday present.

When she opened the box, she hugged me like I had handed her gold, and on day one she outwalked both of us across Prague. Up the hill to Prague Castle, across the Charles Bridge, through every cobblestone street in the Old Town. At the top of a stone lookout, she made Sophie take her picture with both arms up, like she won a race.

Day two, she was noticeably slower on the stairs, sitting down on a stone bench while Sophie and I looked at postcards.

Day three, everything changed.

She announced she would skip the castle tour, claiming it was just a touch of jet lag. She waited at a café at the bottom of the hill for four hours.

After that morning, she stopped leaving the hotel room after lunch, telling us to go ahead while she rested up for dinner.

On the tour bus to Český Krumlov, she stayed in her seat while the group walked up to the castle. She told the guide she had seen enough castles. My mother had talked about that castle for three months.

And at dinner on our last night, Sophie asked if Grandma wanted to see the bridge lit up.

My mom snapped at her. Really snapped.

Sophie put her fork down, ran to the bathroom, and cried.

My mom does not snap, and in 14 years I had never heard her raise her voice to Sophie.

I thought she was tired. I thought, honestly, maybe this is just what 68 looks like, and I had waited too long to take her.

I was wrong about all of it.

That night at one in the morning, I heard water running in our hotel bathroom.

Mom was sitting on the edge of the tub with both feet in the sink under cold tap water, with the ice bucket from the hallway dumped in.

A plastic bottle of ibuprofen was sitting on the counter, and more than half of it was already gone after just five days in Europe.

Her feet were red and puffy over the tops of her socks. There was a blister on each heel the size of a quarter, and a deep red line across her toes where the shoe had pressed all day.

She admitted her feet had started aching on the second afternoon and never stopped throbbing, and by lunch every day the shoes felt two sizes too small. Every morning, the first steps on the stone floor felt like walking on gravel.

She had not said a word because I spent so much on the trip. And on the shoes.

"They're the good ones," she said. "You bought me the good ones. I didn't want you to think it was the shoes."

The next morning, a woman on our tour sat down at our breakfast table. Her name was Bea. She was 77, a widow from Erie, Pennsylvania, on her eleventh trip to Europe.

She walked every hill on that tour and was always first back to the bus.

Bea watched my mom gingerly ease into a wooden breakfast chair, wincing with every inch, and asked one simple question:

"What are you walking in, hon?"

Mom pointed sheepishly under the table, so Bea reached down, picked up one of the Hokas, turned it over in her hands, and chuckled softly. Not a mean laugh.

"These were made for running down a flat road for an hour," Bea said. "You're on old stones for eight hours. Standing in lines, sitting on buses, climbing castle hills. Different job."

Then Bea showed us three things.

First, she pulled the insole out of the shoe and pressed her thumb firmly into the heel cushion.

The dent stayed.

"Foam," Bea said. "Feels like a cloud in the store. By lunch it's packed flat under you, and it doesn't come back until you take them off at night. So you have the least support right when you need it most."

Second, Bea asked my mother exactly what time of day the shoes started pinching her toes.

"Right around two or three in the afternoon," Mom admitted.

"Of course," Bea said. "Feet swell on a long day. Up to half a size. A shoe that fits at breakfast is too small by three o'clock unless the top gives."

Third, Bea placed my mom's Hoka in my left hand, and slipped off her own shoe and placed it in my right hand.

Mom's shoe felt like an actual brick.

"You lift that twenty thousand times a day," Bea said. "Your feet aren't the only thing paying for it. Ask your knees."

Then Bea said the thing I wrote on the back of our tour map:

"On stones you need three things. Arch support built into the shoe, so there's nothing to pack flat. A top that stretches when your feet swell. And light."

Then she added: "Mine cost less than half of those."

On the bus that afternoon, Bea told me something I still think about. Most of the women she started traveling with twenty years ago do not travel anymore.

"Not because of their age," she said. "Because of their feet. And nobody ever told them it was the shoes."

Mom wore old canvas slip-ons for the rest of the trip and sat out the river walk on our last day.

I have never felt worse about a gift.

When we got home, I bought her a $45 pair of memory-foam walking shoes at a big store. Soft as pillows.

That Saturday she wore them to the farmers market, and that night I pressed my thumb into the heel cushion.

Pancake. It was the exact same cheap foam, just sold at a lower price point.

I checked the other shoes in her closet against Bea's tests.

The Dansko clogs she wore in the cafeteria cost $160. Rock hard from day one, and heaviest of all. Her $75 Skechers memory-foam pairs were crushed flat. I checked popular runners online: Brooks Ghost at $140 had a narrow tapered toe box that pinched toes. On Cloud at $180 had a tight triangle toe box that squeezed by lunch. Vionic at $130 had a rigid mold with one fixed shape that couldn't handle afternoon swelling.

Every single one failed Bea's three tests.

Only one shoe passed all three tests on my board.

Comfort Wear. Their Ortho Stretch Cushion Shoe. It was on sale for $59.95.

The sixty-dollar pair outscored the one hundred sixty-five dollar Hokas on every single feature test.

The arch support is built directly into the structure of the shoe, so there is no foam insert to pack down. The upper is a breathable stretch knit that gives when your feet swell. It is light. The sole is firm enough for uneven stones but still bends, the toe box is wide, and the rubber tread grips wet ground.

It is not a flimsy knit sock with a sole. It has real support from heel to toe.

When the box arrived, Mom held them up and said, "These are sixty dollars?" She was skeptical after the expensive pair failed.

During the first week, she reported that they felt noticeably firmer than the Hokas with far less squish, calling them steady and fine.

She didn't feel a miracle overnight. But the step counter does not lie.

Week 1: 4,100 steps a day. The mailbox and her flower beds.

Week 3: 9,800 steps a day. Back at the Saturday farmers market. She walked both sides of the street and carried her own bags.

Week 6: 14,600 steps at the zoo with Sophie. That night, no ice bucket. No pills on the counter. She texted me a picture of her feet up on the couch with a thumbs-up.

Six months after Prague, Mom called me on a Tuesday morning.

"Pack your bags. We're going back."

Last month, we landed back in the Czech Republic.

Day one, Mom walked all the way up the hill to Prague Castle. At the stone lookout over the red roofs, she made Sophie take her picture with both arms up again.

In Český Krumlov, she did not stay on the bus. She walked the cobblestone path all the way up to the castle gates. She held Sophie's hand on the steep part, insisting it was for Sophie's balance, not hers.

Her step count that day: 18,227 steps.

My feet hurt more than hers did.

That night at dinner, Mom apologized to Sophie for snapping at her the year before. Then she ordered dessert for the table and told the waiter it was her second time in his country, and it would not be her last.
