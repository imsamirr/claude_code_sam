## PRIMARY TEXT

There's a plastic bin in my garage with $1,140 worth of nurse shoes in it.

My husband put a strip of tape on the lid. It says THE GRAVEYARD.

Every pair in there was "the one." Every pair in there was dead within two months.

And six months ago, I almost let that bin end my career.

I'm Marisol. I've been an ER nurse for 16 years. Three 12s a week, sometimes four when we're short, which is always.

If you work 12s, you already know the bin. You probably have one.

Here's mine.

$180 for Hokas, because every nurse I know swore by them.
$150 for Brooks, because a running store guy watched me walk for ten seconds and said "you overpronate."
$200 for Danskos, because the old-school nurses said it's clogs or nothing.
$70 for Skechers, when I was broke and desperate.
Two more pairs of Hokas, because I thought the first ones must have been defective.

It went the same way every time. Week one: heaven. Month two: by hour four my heels were on fire, by hour eight my back was locking up, and by hour twelve I was walking to my car like I'd been in a fight.

I started taking ibuprofen in the parking garage before every shift. Like a ritual. Coffee, badge, two pills.

My feet would swell so much that by 3 a.m. my toes were jammed against the front of the shoe like sausages in a casing. I'd go into the supply room and loosen my laces just to breathe.

And I kept buying shoes. Because what else do you do?

The night it broke me was a Tuesday.

We had a code around 4 a.m. I was running down the hallway and hit the corner by bay six, where environmental services had just mopped.

My shoe went out from under me.

I caught myself on the crash cart with one hand. Twisted my knee. Didn't fall. Kept going, because that's the job.

But all I could think after was: one more inch and I'm not the nurse in that room. I'm the patient.

At 7:40 that morning I sat in my car in the parking garage. I took both shoes off, put my feet on the dashboard, and cried so hard I fogged the windshield.

Then I opened my laptop right there and filled out an application for a Monday-to-Friday clinic job. Sitting down. Fourteen thousand dollars less a year.

I saved it in my drafts. I told myself I'd send it after my next stretch.

I didn't send it. Because of Deb.

Deb is our night charge nurse. She's 61. She's done twelve-hour shifts for 34 years. I have worked beside that woman for a decade and I don't think I have ever seen her sit down.

The next night she watched me limp in from the parking garage and pulled me into the med room.

"What's going on with your feet?"

I told her everything. The bin. The ibuprofen. The corner by bay six. The application in my drafts.

She looked down at my shoes, the newest Hokas, $180, three weeks old, and said one sentence I'll never forget:

"Honey, you're wearing a running shoe to a standing job."

Then she said, "It's not the brand. It's the design."

And she explained it to me right there between the Pyxis and the sharps container.

Hokas, Brooks, On Clouds, all of them are built to move a runner forward for forty minutes. That big soft foam is tuned to spring you through a stride.

We are not striding. We are standing. Charting. Holding a limb. Doing compressions. Waiting on a doctor. Standing still on concrete for twelve hours.

When you stand still on running foam, nothing springs. Your weight just sits on it and squashes it. It feels incredible the first week. Then it packs flat by hour four, so you've got the least support of the whole day right when your feet hurt the most.

"That's your heel by midnight," she said.

Then the grip. Running shoes are made to grab dry pavement. Not a hospital floor somebody mopped ten minutes ago.

"That's your corner by bay six."

Then the toe box. Narrow, and it doesn't stretch. Your feet swell up to half a size over a twelve. So the shoe that fit at 7 p.m. is squeezing you by 3 a.m.

"That's your sausages."

I just stood there. Every single problem I'd blamed on my body, my age, my "bad feet," she'd just explained in under two minutes. And none of it was about me. All of it was about the shoe.

Then she said the thing that actually made me angry:

"Most of the 'nurse shoes' out there? Somebody took a running shoe and slapped a sticker on it that says *approved for work.* Same foam. Same narrow toe. Higher price."

Six pairs. $1,140. And I'd basically bought the same wrong shoe six times with six different logos.

I asked her what she wears.

She lifted her foot onto the step stool.

"These. Comfort Wear. I've been rotating two pairs for two years."

Black with purple. I'd never heard of them.

I'll be honest, my first reaction was an eye roll. I had bought "the nurse shoe" six times. I wasn't in a hurry to make it seven.

So that night I did something I'd never done before. Instead of buying a shoe because someone loved it, I checked it against what Deb said a shift actually does to your feet.

**Cushioning made for standing, not running.** Check. Comfort Wear doesn't make shoes for athletes. They make them for people who stand for a living.

**An arch that's structural, not foam.** It's built right into the shoe, so there's nothing to pack flat at hour four.

**A wide toe box with a stretch knit upper.** It actually widens as your feet swell, instead of pressing back.

**Slip-resistant grip for wet floors.** For the corner by bay six.

And around $60. Less than a third of what I'd paid for the Hokas sitting on my feet.

I ordered a pair. Black Purple, because that's what Deb had.

**First shift:** I kept waiting for it. Hour four, the heel. Nothing. Hour eight, the back. Nothing. At hour twelve I walked to my car like a normal person. I sat in the driver's seat and realized I hadn't taken my shoes off.

**Week two:** I forgot the ibuprofen in the parking garage. Then I forgot it again. Then I noticed I'd stopped reaching for it.

**Month one:** my back, which had been talking to me for three years, had gone quiet. I did four 12s in a row during a short-staffed week. Four. Zero pain.

**Month two:** our unit manager posted an extra shift on a Saturday. Old me would've pretended I didn't see it. New me took it, because I felt good enough to.

That one extra shift paid for my shoes four times over.

It's been seven months now. I rotate two pairs, Black Purple and White Blue, exactly like Deb. They still feel like they did on day one.

Nothing new has gone into the graveyard.

And the clinic application?

It's still in my drafts. I'm never going to send it.
