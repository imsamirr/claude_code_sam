## PRIMARY TEXT

Last spring my 9-year-old asked me a question at dinner that I still can't shake.

"Mom, why are you nicer at school than you are at home?"

She wasn't being mean. She was genuinely curious. Her best friend is in the class next door to mine, and she'd heard I was "the fun teacher."

At home, I was the mom lying on the kitchen floor at 4 p.m. with her legs up the wall, saying "give me twenty minutes" for the third hour in a row.

I gave twenty-six other kids everything I had, every single day. By the time I got home to my own kid, there was nothing left.

I'm writing this for every teacher who knows exactly what I mean.

My name is Jen. I've taught third grade for 21 years. I love it. I'd never do anything else.

But somewhere around year fifteen my feet started sending the bill.

Heels on fire by lunch. A bunion on my left foot that throbbed by sixth period. A lower back that locked up on hallway duty. And by dismissal, my feet were so swollen that my "good" shoes felt like they'd shrunk.

I did what every teacher does. I tried everything the staff room recommended.

The running shoes the guy at the running store sold me. The big cushioned ones everybody's wearing. The clogs the veteran teachers swear by. The orthopedic brand with the podiatrist's name on it. Birkenstocks, because the art teacher looks so comfortable. Even a pair of Nikes, because at least they looked cute with jeans on Fridays.

Every pair felt fine in the store. Every pair had quit on me by March.

I figured that's just teaching. You give your feet to the job like you give your weekends to grading.

Then, one icy morning in January, I brought a coffee out to Mrs. Odette.

Mrs. Odette is our crossing guard. She's 68. She stands on that corner for two hours every morning and two hours every afternoon, in every kind of weather, and she has for twelve years.

She took the coffee, looked at me walking back up the sidewalk, and said:

"You're walking like a teacher in March, and it's January."

I laughed and told her my feet were shot. Too much walking.

She shook her head.

"Sweetheart, your feet aren't wrecked from walking. They're wrecked from standing."

I'd never thought about it that way. But she was right.

Think about an actual school day. You're not strolling. You're planted. At the whiteboard. Bent over a student's desk. Posted on hallway duty. Standing at the door for arrivals. Hours of it, on tile, linoleum and concrete, the hardest floors in the building.

"That's what I do out here," she said. "I stand. And almost no shoe is built for standing. They're built for walking, or running. Different job."

Then she told me what goes wrong.

Running foam is made to spring you forward into the next step. When you stand still, nothing springs. Your weight just sits there and presses it flat. By lunch, it's done.

Your feet swell over a long day. A stiff or narrow shoe doesn't swell with you. So by dismissal it's squeezing a bigger foot than the one you put in it.

And a lot of "supportive" shoes are so rigid, or so heavy, that you feel them every second.

I went home that night and did the most teacher thing possible.

I lined up every pair in my closet and graded them.

One criterion. A full school day, first bell to last.

I used my red pen. I'm not proud of how satisfying that was.

**Nike Air Max. $130. Score: 5.1.**
Let's be honest, it's a fashion shoe. That air bubble is there for the look, not your arch. Under my foot it's nearly flat. By third period my arch was doing all the work by itself.

**Birkenstocks. $110. Score: 5.9.**
The cork really does hold an arch. But cork takes the shape of your foot and none of the impact, so there's nothing between you and the linoleum. And they're backless, so my toes clenched all day just to keep them on. Plus they slap when you walk. During silent reading. Every time.

**Brooks. $140. Score: 6.8.**
A wonderful shoe for runners. That's the problem. The toe box is narrow and tapers to a point, and it pressed my bunion exactly where it hurts. I found a hole worn clean through the side of my old pair, right where the bunion sits.

**Hoka. $165.**
Heaven through first period. But the taller and softer the sole, the less steady you are the second you stop and crouch down to a student's desk. I wobbled every time. And that plush foam packs down under a full day of standing. A few months in, it was a flatter, deader shoe than the one I bought.

**Dansko clogs.**
Genuinely built for people who stand. But that footbed is rock hard from day one, and they were the heaviest pair I own. I was aware of my shoes all day long.

**Vionic. $130. Score: 7.5.**
The podiatrist-designed brand. The one I really expected to win. And there IS real arch support in there. But it's a rigid, molded footbed, one fixed shape. My feet don't hold one shape across a school day. They swell. So the support that fit me at 8 a.m. was pressing into a bigger foot by 3.

Not one A in the bunch. I'd spent over $800 on a closet full of C's and D's.

The next morning I brought Mrs. Odette another coffee. Then I asked the obvious question.

"What do YOU wear out here?"

She stuck her foot out from under her orange vest.

"Comfort Wear. The stretchy ones. My daughter's a nurse. She got me onto them."

Navy blue. Normal-looking sneakers. I'd never heard of the brand.

"About sixty bucks," she said. "Grade them yourself."

So I did.

I ordered a pair and gave them the same test. Two full weeks of school days. Same rubric. Same red pen.

**The arch support is built right into the shoe.** It's structural, not foam. At the last bell it was holding me up the same way it did at the first.

**The sole** is firm enough to protect my arches from the concrete, but flexible enough to move fast when a kid needs me across the room. Crouching at desks, I didn't wobble once.

**The knit upper stretches** as my feet swell through the day, instead of pressing back. My bunion finally had room. By dismissal, they still fit like they did at 8 a.m.

**The tread grips** the hallway right after the custodian waxes it.

**They're light.** Hall duty stopped feeling like leg day.

**And they look like a clean, normal sneaker.** Not athletic. Not orthopedic. Polished enough for parent-teacher night.

Final score: 9.3. The highest grade on my rubric, at the lowest price on it.

A $60 shoe outscored the one designed by podiatrists. Not because it's fancy. Because it's the only one in my closet that was actually built for my day. Comfort Wear doesn't make shoes for runners. They make them for people who stand on hard floors for a living: nurses, teachers, crossing guards.

Here's what changed after those two weeks. And it wasn't really about my feet.

I got to the last bell with something left.

**Week one:** I noticed I wasn't counting down the minutes to sit during my planning period.

**Week two:** I stayed late for the science fair and stood at the tables for two straight hours. I drove home and didn't lie on the kitchen floor.

**Week four:** parent-teacher night. Three hours on my feet, in shoes that looked nice with my dress pants. One mom asked where I got them.

**Week six:** my daughter asked me to play four square in the driveway after school. Old me would have said "give me twenty minutes."

I said, "You're going down."

We played until the streetlights came on.

A few weeks later, at dinner, she said it, just out of nowhere:

"You're fun at home again."

I had to leave the table for a second.

My old rubric is still taped inside my closet door. There's an A at the bottom of it now, circled twice in red pen.

And Mrs. Odette gets her coffee every morning. Forever.
