## PRIMARY TEXT

On the third night of our 40th anniversary trip, my husband texted me a picture from the top of the hill.

The castle wall. The whole city glowing orange underneath it. The river. The sunset.

Four words: "Wish you were here."

I was 200 feet below him, sitting at a café table at the bottom of the hill, with my shoes off.

I'd been planning that trip for three years. And I watched the best part of it on a phone screen.

If you've got a trip to Europe coming up, I want to tell you what put me in that chair. It wasn't my age, and it wasn't my feet. It was the worst piece of travel advice there is, and I took it.

"Just wear your comfortable sneakers."

My name is Linda. I'm 64. I was a dental hygienist for 31 years, so I know what it means to be on my feet. My husband Tom and I got married in 1986, and for our 40th we finally did it: two weeks in Portugal. Lisbon, then Porto.

I prepared like it was a final exam. I walked three miles a day for two months. I read every travel blog. Every single one said the same thing. "Wear your most comfortable sneakers."

So I packed my Hokas, the big cushioned ones everybody raves about. $165. I packed a pair of Nikes as a backup, because they looked nicer for dinner. And for the flight I wore memory foam slip-ons, because they're easy at security.

That was my first mistake.

Nine hours on a plane, and my feet swelled like they always do on long flights. By the time we landed, the slip-ons felt two sizes too small. We dropped our bags and went straight out to explore, because who sleeps on day one?

Memory foam squashes under your weight and doesn't come back until you take the shoes off. By that first afternoon, climbing those old hillside staircases, I was standing on nothing.

Fine, I thought. Tomorrow I'll wear the Hokas.

If you've never been to Lisbon, the sidewalks are made of little cobblestones. Thousands of them, worn smooth and shiny over hundreds of years. They're gorgeous. And they're not flat. Not even close.

In the Hokas, every step rolled my foot a little to one side. That big soft sole was made to cushion your foot going straight down on a flat road. On those stones it just wobbled. And they're heavy, almost twice the weight of a normal shoe. Up those hills, my legs felt like I was dragging ankle weights.

By the morning of day two, I had a stabbing pain in my right heel, like stepping on a tack every time I put it down. By that evening, my knees hurt more than my feet.

Day three, I switched to the Nikes.

By 3 p.m. my feet were so swollen the toe box was crushing my toes together. Nike built those for running tracks and gym floors. Not 400-year-old streets.

That evening, Tom wanted to walk up to the castle for sunset. It was the one thing he'd talked about for three years.

I got about a third of the way up. Then I sat down at a café on the corner and told him, "Go. Take pictures. I'll be right here."

And at 7:42 p.m., my phone buzzed.

"Wish you were here."

I texted back: "I'm 200 feet below you. In my socks."

I tried to make it sound funny. It wasn't funny. I sat there and cried into a glass of wine I didn't want.

A woman at the next table leaned over and handed me a napkin.

She was American, maybe 55, in scrubs-colored leggings and a ball cap. She'd just come down the same hill I couldn't get up.

"First trip to Lisbon?" she asked.

I nodded.

"Let me guess. Hokas?"

I actually laughed. "How did you know?"

"Because I'm a travel nurse," she said. "I see this every week. These streets eat running shoes alive."

Her name was Carol. She'd done a 13-week contract in Boston and was spending her time off walking Europe. She told me she'd done 50,000 steps in two days in Barcelona the week before, without a hint of pain.

Then she explained what had happened to my feet. And honestly, it was like a light switched on.

"Running shoes are built for smooth surfaces and short bursts," she said. "Travel is the exact opposite. You're walking eight or ten miles a day on stone you've never touched before. Up hills. Down staircases. Standing in museum lines."

All that thick foam is made to absorb impact going straight down, like running on a flat road. On cobblestones your foot rolls side to side with every step, and the soft foam gives you nothing for that. So you're actually less stable than you'd be in a flatter shoe.

The heavy sole? You're lifting it 20,000 times a day, uphill.

The narrow toe box? Your feet swell on the flight and keep swelling all day. By 3 p.m. they've got nowhere to go.

And memory foam is the worst of all. It gives up by early afternoon, which is exactly when you're climbing to the view you flew nine hours to see.

"That's your heel," she said, pointing at my foot. "That's your knees. That's why you're down here and your husband's up there."

Then she lifted her foot onto the chair next to me.

"I wear the same shoes I wear on 12-hour shifts. If they hold up through a 12 on hospital floors, they hold up through anything."

They were from a company called Comfort Wear. The Ortho Stretch Cushion Shoe. I'd never heard of it.

She ran through it like a checklist.

**The sole** is firm enough to protect you from cobblestones, but flexible enough that you're not clomping around like you're in hiking boots.

**The toe box is wide** and the top is a stretch knit, so your toes can spread out, and it gives as your feet swell on the flight and through the day.

**The arch support doesn't quit at 2 p.m.** It's built into the shoe, so there's no foam to collapse.

**They're light.** You forget you're wearing them by mile six.

**And they look nice** enough to walk straight into a restaurant.

"About sixty bucks," she said. "Comfort Wear doesn't make shoes for athletes. They make them for people whose feet have to survive real life. Nurses. And people who saved up for a trip like this one."

I wrote the name on the napkin she'd handed me. I still have it.

I couldn't get a pair in Portugal. So I spent the rest of the trip being careful. Short days. A lot of cafés. Porto mostly from a taxi window.

On the flight home, Tom held my hand and said, "We'll come back and do the castle."

I didn't believe him. I figured that was it, that I'd aged out of trips like this.

The day we got home, I ordered a pair.

I'll be honest about the first week. They felt firmer than my cushy Hokas, and I wasn't sure. By the second week I was walking the steep hill behind our church every morning, and my heel stopped talking to me. By the fourth week I did a full day at the city art museum, eight hours on hard floors, and came home and forgot to take them off.

So in May, Tom booked it again. Lisbon. "To finish the trip," he said.

I wore my Comfort Wear on the flight. Nine hours. When we landed, my feet had swelled like they always do, but the knit just moved with them. No squeezing.

Day one: 22,000 steps through the old city center, up the staircases and across the cobblestones. Walking back from dinner along the river at 10 p.m., I realized I hadn't thought about my feet once.

Day two: no stabbing heel when I got out of bed. My first step was just a step.

Day three: the castle.

I walked every step of that hill. All the way to the top. We got there twenty minutes before sunset.

My feet didn't hurt less.

They didn't hurt.

Tom took a picture of me against the castle wall with the whole city glowing behind me. Then he did something that made me laugh so hard a tourist turned around.

He texted it to me. I was standing right next to him.

Three words this time.

"You're here. ❤️"

It's my phone background now.

A few months later, my sister flew to Paris. I sent her a pair before she left. She called me from a café near the river on day four just to say thank you. She said she was the only one in her tour group who wasn't limping.
