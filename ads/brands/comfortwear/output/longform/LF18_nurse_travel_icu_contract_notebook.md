## PRIMARY TEXT

Inside my travel bag is a black spiral notebook where I have logged nine years of 13-week ICU contracts, five hospitals, and eight hundred and forty-five dollars in dead work shoes.

Contract 18 in Denver: Hoka Bondi, $165. Score: 4 out of 10. Hour eight: arches aching.

Contract 20 in Phoenix: Dansko clogs, $150. Score: 3 out of 10. Hour four: rock hard.

Contract 22 in Boston: Brooks Ghost, $140. Score: 5 out of 10. Hour six: pinched toes.

Contract 24 in Minneapolis: On Cloud, $180. Score: 4 out of 10. Hour seven: feet suffocated.

Contract 25 in Cleveland: The sixty-dollar pair, $59.95. Score: 10 out of 10. Hour twelve: arches held.

It took nine winters on the road, a divorce, and a veteran respiratory therapist in Minneapolis named Gloria to show me why the cheapest pair on my board won the whole board.

Let me explain.

I am forty-four. I have been a travel ICU nurse for nine years. I pack my entire life into two large suitcases, an emergency kit, and the trunk of my rental car every thirteen weeks.

If you have ever worked in an intensive care unit, you know you do not sit down.

You are on your feet on hard tile over concrete for twelve straight hours. You are running code blues, titrating six continuous IV drips at once, turning two-hundred-and-fifty-pound patients, and walking twelve miles a shift between patient rooms and the supply bay.

Every time I signed a new contract, I bought whatever shoe the nurses on that unit swore by.

In Denver, everybody was wearing Hokas. I dropped a hundred and sixty-five dollars on a pair of Bondi runners. They felt great for two hours in the store. But by hour eight of my night shift, the thick foam packed flat under my body weight. My arches had zero support left, and my plantar fascia stretched tight like a wire.

In Phoenix, the older nurses told me to get Dansko clogs. I spent a hundred and fifty dollars. They were rock hard from day one. It felt like strapping two solid two-by-fours to the bottoms of my feet. My shins and knees burned by 11 p.m.

In Boston, I spent a hundred and forty dollars on Brooks Ghost because a charge nurse promised they were the best. But they are built to push a runner forward on an outdoor track. The toe box tapers down so narrow that my pinky toes were red and numb before my shift was half over.

In Minneapolis, I bought a hundred and eighty dollar pair of On Clouds. They had that triangle-shaped toe box that squeezed the front of my feet like a vice by lunch.

I even tried an eighty-dollar pair of Skechers with memory foam. That foam compressed flat under my body weight before midnight and never recovered until I took them off at home. I tried a hundred and thirty dollar pair of Vionics with a rigid orthotic footbed, but feet swell during a shift, so that hard arch dug into my foot like a wooden wedge.

By my fourth year traveling, I had a graveyard of expensive shoes in the trunk of my car.

Eight hundred and forty-five dollars in shoes, and every single contract ended the same way.

Sitting in my freezing rental car in the hospital parking lot at 7:30 in the morning, massaging my aching arches over a frozen water bottle before I could even drive back to my temporary apartment.

Then this past summer, on contract twenty-four in Minneapolis, I met Gloria.

Gloria was fifty-eight. She was a night-shift respiratory therapist who had worked ICU and step-down units for twenty-six years.

At four in the morning during a heavy shift, she walked into the central charting station and saw me leaning hard against the counter, shifting my weight from one foot to the other.

Gloria set down her clipboard, looked down at my shoes, and shook her head.

"You bought the runner hype, didn't you?" she asked.

"Everyone on this floor wears them," I said. "They cost a hundred and eighty dollars."

Gloria pulled an empty rolling stool over, sat down, and told me to take one shoe off.

She pulled the insole out and laid it flat on the desk. Then she pressed her thumb hard into the center of the arch and heel.

The foam compressed flat and stayed dented. It did not bounce back.

"Look at that," Gloria said. "Running shoes are built for a runner doing a 5k on asphalt. That foam is tuned to spring you forward through a running stride. It is not engineered to hold a nurse standing on concrete for twelve hours straight."

Then she broke down what was happening to my feet:

First, soft foam packs flat under your body weight.

"Memory foam and running foam are full of air bubbles," Gloria said. "They feel soft like pillows in the store. But by hour eight of your shift, that foam is crushed flat. The arch has nothing under it, and your heel takes every single step directly on the concrete floor."

Second, feet swell during a twelve-hour shift.

"When you stand all night, gravity pulls fluid down," she said. "Your feet swell up to half a shoe size. A shoe with a fixed, rigid upper that fits at breakfast squeezes your foot like a tourniquet by three in the morning."

Third, heavy shoes destroy your joints.

"Lift a heavy shoe twenty thousand times a shift, and your knees and back pay for it."

Then Gloria pointed down at her own plain black sneakers.

"You need three things for this floor," she said. "Arch support built directly into the base of the shoe so it cannot pack flat. A stretch-knit upper that gives when your feet swell. And light."

Then she told me what she was wearing:

Comfort Wear, the Ortho Stretch Cushion Shoe.

She told me they were $59.95.

I will admit, when she told me the price, I was skeptical.

I had spent a hundred and eighty dollars on On Clouds and a hundred and sixty-five dollars on Hokas. How could a sixty-dollar shoe outperform shoes that cost three times as much?

I worried they would be flimsy knit socks with cheap rubber glued on. Or that they would fall apart in two weeks like forty-dollar supermarket copies.

Gloria laughed. "Order a pair. They give you a full thirty-day money-back guarantee. If they do not hold your arches, you get every penny back."

When I got to my rental apartment that morning, I ordered a pair in black.

When my next contract started in Cleveland this past September, cold rain and wind were already coming down off Lake Erie.

I packed the Comfort Wear shoes in my bag.

The first night in Cleveland, my first reaction was honest: they felt firmer than my Hokas. There was less squishy bounce. They felt solid and steady under my feet.

At hour four, my arches were fine.

At hour eight, the time when my arches normally started screaming in running shoes, I was running between two trauma rooms hanging blood.

At hour ten, I stopped at the medication cart and realized something that stopped me in my tracks:

I had not thought about my feet once all night.

The built-in arch support was holding its shape. It had not compressed flat. The stretch-knit top was giving naturally as my feet swelled, with zero pinching across my toes. And the firm sole gripped the wet linoleum when someone spilled saline in the hallway.

By week four of my Cleveland contract, the frozen water bottle in my rental car was gone.

By week eight, I was walking out to my car at 7:30 in the morning without limping across the gravel parking lot.

And on week thirteen, my final week of the Cleveland contract, our unit manager came up to me on Friday morning.

Two nurses had called in sick with the flu. She asked if anyone could pick up an extra twelve-hour night shift on Saturday on crisis overtime.

For nine years as a travel nurse, I had one strict rule: never pick up an extra shift on week thirteen. By the end of a contract, my arches were so beat up that I needed three full days in bed just to recover.

I looked down at my shoes. My feet felt completely fine.

I picked up the shift.

I picked up an extra shift on week thirteen because I felt good enough to do it.

That night, I opened my black notebook and wrote down the final score for Contract 25:

Comfort Wear: 10 out of 10. Price: $59.95.

The highest score on my board at the lowest price on it. Sixty dollars outscored the hundred and eighty dollar pair.
