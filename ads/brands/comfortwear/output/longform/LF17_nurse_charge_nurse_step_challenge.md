## PRIMARY TEXT

I was pinning the new hospital wellness challenge leaderboard to the breakroom corkboard when I saw where Brenda ranked.

Week one: 1st place, 94,120 steps.

Week three: 8th place, 58,400 steps.

Week five: dead last, 14,210 steps.

It took an IV pump she snapped at me over, an open door to the med room at two in the morning, and a retired nurse in the cafeteria to find out what happened to the woman who built 4-West.

Brenda never said a word to anyone. She was always the strong one.

Let me back up.

I am twenty-six. This is my first year as a registered nurse on a thirty-bed med-surg floor. If you have ever worked in a hospital, you know every unit has one person who holds the entire floor together.

On 4-West, that person is Brenda.

She is fifty-nine. She has walked these concrete and linoleum hallways for thirty-one years. She trained our nurse manager. She trained our charge nurses. She trained me when I was terrified of my own shadow during orientation.

Brenda never took an extra break. She never complained about bad assignments. And for four years straight, she won the hospital wellness step challenge without even trying.

She logged twenty thousand steps a shift just keeping our patients alive.

Then, around the middle of September, little things started slipping.

First, she stopped walking down the hall to check on the new grads. She started doing handoffs from the rolling desk chair at the central station.

Then she started asking the unit techs to run down to the lab for blood tubes she used to grab herself.

Two weeks later, during a hectic Tuesday night shift, an IV pump in room 412 started beeping. I could not get the occlusion alarm to clear. I asked Brenda for help, and she snapped at me.

"Figure it out, Claire. You have a license. I cannot keep walking back and forth for every little alarm."

She had never raised her voice at me once. Not even when I dropped an entire tray of sterile supplies my second week.

I went into the clean utility room and cried for two minutes. I thought she was fed up with me. I thought she was burned out on nursing.

I was wrong about both.

The real answer came three days later, at two in the morning.

We had a patient spike a fever, and I needed acetaminophen from the locked medication room behind the nurses station.

The door was cracked open an inch. The light was off, but the amber glow from the automated drug dispenser was shining across the floor.

I pushed the door open.

Brenda was sitting on an overturned gray plastic laundry bin wedged between the Pyxis machine and the IV fluid shelves.

Her shoes were off on the tile.

Her bare feet were resting on two frozen one-liter bags of normal saline she had pulled from the unit fridge, wrapped in blue disposable pads.

I stood there frozen.

Her heels were dark red and swollen tight. When she saw me, her face went completely white. For three seconds neither of us said anything.

Then she looked down at the floor and whispered:

"Please do not tell the floor, Claire. I am the charge nurse. If I cannot walk this unit, they will pull me off bedside."

She told me her doctor had diagnosed her with plantar fasciitis six months earlier. She had not told a soul.

Every morning, the first steps out of bed felt like stepping flat on broken glass. She would grip the hallway wall in her own house just to reach the bathroom.

By hour four of her shift on the concrete hospital floors, the cushion under her heels was completely gone. Every single step hammered straight into the bone.

By hour eight, she was hiding in the med room between med passes just to get off her feet for sixty seconds.

I looked down at the shoes sitting next to the laundry bin.

They were a pair of Hokas. Big, thick, marshmallow running soles. A hundred and sixty-five dollars.

"I bought the ones everyone recommended," Brenda said, her voice shaking. "I spent a hundred and sixty-five dollars on those Hokas. They felt great for two hours in the store, but by hour four the foam packs flat and my foot rolls side to side when I turn into a room. And they are almost twice the weight of regular shoes, so my knees ache as much as my heels."

She pointed to the locker bank behind the station.

"In my locker I have a hundred and forty dollar pair of Brooks Ghost. The toe box tapers down so narrow that my pinky toes go numb by noon. At home in my closet I have a hundred and eighty dollar pair of On Clouds with that triangle-shaped toe box that squeezes like a vice by lunch. I have a hundred and sixty dollar pair of Dansko clogs that were rock hard from day one. And last year I spent eighty dollars on Skechers memory foam that flattened out under my weight before my first lunch break and never bounced back."

She rubbed the side of her heel.

"I spent hundreds of dollars on the good ones. Everyone wears them. My feet just quit."

I could not stop thinking about that laundry bin.

The next morning after shift change, I saw Marsha sitting alone in the hospital cafeteria.

Marsha is sixty-two. She worked med-surg on 4-West for twenty-eight years before transferring down to telephone triage two years ago.

I sat down across from her and asked why she had left the floor.

Marsha did not hesitate.

"My feet," she said. "I loved the patients. I loved the team. But my heels were burning so bad by seven in the morning that I was sitting on the edge of the bed dreading to stand up. Nobody ever told me it was the shoes."

Then Marsha pulled a pen from her scrub pocket and laid it on the cafeteria table.

"You kids all wear running shoes now," Marsha said. "Running shoes are built for a runner doing a 5k on asphalt. They are tuned to spring a stride forward for forty-five minutes. They are not built to hold a nurse standing on linoleum over concrete for twelve hours."

Then she explained the three things that happen to a nurse's foot:

First, running foam packs flat under body weight.

"Memory foam and running foam are full of tiny air pockets," Marsha said. "They feel like pillows when you try them on at the shoe store. But your body weight compresses those bubbles continuously. By hour four of a shift, that foam is packed flat like cardboard. The arch has nothing under it, and your heel takes every step directly on the concrete."

Second, feet swell during the shift.

"A nurse's foot swells up to half a shoe size by mid-shift," she said. "When you stand for twelve hours, gravity pulls fluid down. A shoe with a fixed, stiff upper that fits at seven in the morning squeezes your toes and the top of your foot like a clamp by three in the afternoon."

Third, heavy shoes drain your legs.

"A heavy clog or a bulky max-cushion sneaker feels fine for ten steps," Marsha said. "Lift that heavy shoe twenty thousand times a shift, and your knees and shins pay for it."

Then Marsha wrote four words on a cafeteria napkin:

Built-in arch. Stretch top.

"If you want Brenda to survive on the floor," Marsha said, "find a shoe with arch support built directly into the base so it cannot pack flat. Find a top that stretches when her feet swell. And make sure it is light."

When I got home that afternoon, I tested Brenda's Hokas using Marsha's thumb test.

I took the insole out and pressed my thumb into the center of the heel cup.

The foam sank down and stayed indented. It did not bounce back. It was packed completely flat under the heel bone.

That weekend I tried buying a forty-five dollar pair of big-box store "memory foam arch" walking shoes to see if they were any different.

I wore them for one four-hour errand run. By Sunday night, the insole was paper-thin. Exactly the same soft foam, just cheaper.

So I sat at my laptop and checked dozens of nurse shoes against Marsha's rules.

Most were just thick running foam with clever marketing. The expensive clogs were rock hard and heavy. The cheap knit shoes had no structure at all, just a soft sock glued to a thin rubber bottom.

Then I found Comfort Wear and their Ortho Stretch Cushion Shoe.

They were $59.95.

The arch support is built directly into the structure of the shoe itself. There is no squishy insert to compress, so the arch stays supported through the twelfth hour just like the first.

The upper is a flexible stretch knit that gives naturally as feet swell during a shift, without pinching the toes or squeezing the top of the foot.

The sole is firm and stable, but still flexible. It does not wobble side to side like high-stack running shoes, and it has slip-resistant grip for hospital spills and wet tile floors.

Best of all, they look like clean, normal sneakers, not clunky orthopedic shoes.

I ordered a pair in black for Brenda in her size.

When the package arrived, I brought it to work the next night and set it on Brenda's locker bench before report.

Brenda opened the box and pulled one shoe out.

"They are sixty dollars?" she asked, looking at the receipt slip. "Claire, my Hokas were a hundred and sixty-five dollars. How can a sixty-dollar shoe hold up to twelve hours on this floor?"

I told her about Marsha. I told her about the foam packing flat.

Brenda sighed, unlaced her running shoes, and slipped them on.

The first week, she was cautious.

She told me they felt firmer than her Hokas. "There is less squish," she said after her first twelve hours. "They are not soft like a pillow. But my feet feel steady."

By week two, Brenda was walking down the hall without holding the handrails. She was not leaning against the nurses station counter during report.

Then the step challenge numbers started coming in on the corkboard:

Week one (before Comfort Wear): 14,210 steps (dead last).

Week two: 54,800 steps (7th place).

Week four: 79,200 steps (3rd place).

Week six: 98,450 steps. First place on the entire hospital board.

Last Tuesday, at three in the morning, Brenda walked up behind me at the charting desk and set a large iced coffee on my desk.

She pulled up a chair and sat down next to me.

"Claire," she said softly. "I owe you an apology. Last month when I snapped at you about that IV pump, I was in so much pain I could not see straight. My heels felt like they were on fire. I was not mad at you. I was scared I was losing my job."

She tapped the toe of her black Comfort Wear shoe against the floor.

"I have not put ice on my heels in three weeks," she said. "I get out of bed in the morning and my feet just work. I have not hidden in the med room once."

Then she smiled. "And I am not leaving 4-West anytime soon."
