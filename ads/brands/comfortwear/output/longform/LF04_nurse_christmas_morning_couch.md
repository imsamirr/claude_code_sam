## PRIMARY TEXT

Last Christmas morning, my kids opened their presents while I watched from the couch with my feet in a mixing bowl of ice water.

I was still in my scrubs.

My mother-in-law took a picture and posted it to Facebook.

"Christmas morning! Mama worked all night ❤️"

Forty-one people liked it.

I looked at it for a long time. My kids are on the floor in the wrapping paper. My husband is on his knees putting batteries in something. And I'm in the back of the frame, on the couch, with a salad bowl full of ice on the rug.

That's the moment I knew something had to change.

I'm Renee. I'm 45. I've been a telemetry nurse for 18 years, nights for most of them.

Somebody has to work Christmas Eve. Most years it's me, because the overnight differential is how we pay for Christmas.

I got home at 7:50 that morning. The kids had been up since 6, waiting for me to open anything.

My son is 6. He grabbed my hand at the door and pulled.

"Mom, sit on the floor with us!"

I said, "I'm right here, bud. I can see from the couch."

I couldn't get down on the floor. I knew if I got down there, I wasn't getting back up without help. And I didn't want them to see that.

So I watched Christmas from the couch.

Here's the thing nobody outside this job understands. I don't get to stop.

My husband's hours got cut last spring. My paycheck is the one that keeps the lights on. My daughter needs braces. I can't go part-time. I can't take a desk job for $14,000 less.

So I'd gotten really good at hiding it.

I chart standing on one foot, then the other, like a flamingo.

I volunteer for the rooms closest to the station and say it's because I like being near the monitors.

I keep a pair of slides in my locker so I can change before the walk to my car.

I take two ibuprofen with my coffee at 7 p.m. and two more at 1 a.m., like clockwork.

And at home there's nobody to hide it from except my kids.

They have a name for it. They call it "shift feet."

"Is it a shift feet night?" my daughter asks, when I come home and go straight to the couch.

She's 12. She started making her brother's breakfast on my sleep days. Nobody asked her to. She just started.

My son stopped asking me to play after work. He just goes to his dad now.

I want to be clear: I tried everything a nurse is supposed to try.

The big cushioned running shoes every nurse on my unit swears by. $165. Three pairs in two years.

The clogs the old-school nurses wear. $200. Hard as a rock and heavy enough that my calves ached by midnight.

Gel insoles. Compression socks. Rolling my arch on a frozen water bottle under the desk.

Every pair felt amazing for the first hour.

By hour four, my heels were on fire.

By hour twelve, my toes were jammed against the front of the shoe so hard I'd go numb on the drive home.

I figured that's what 18 years of nights does to a body. I figured I was just wearing out.

Then, the second week of January, I met Mr. Alvarez.

Bed 14. Eighty-two years old. In for a heart rhythm we needed to watch.

Thirty-one years as a mail carrier. Walking routes in every kind of weather.

At 4 a.m. I was standing at the foot of his bed doing my flamingo thing, shifting foot to foot while I checked his monitor.

He watched me for a minute. Then he said:

"You're standing like a carrier in his first winter."

I laughed and told him my feet were shot. Too much walking.

He shook his head.

"Mija, nobody's feet are wrecked from walking. They're wrecked from standing. Walking, your weight keeps moving. Standing, it just sits there and grinds."

Then he pointed at my shoes.

"Those are running shoes."

I said they're the ones everybody wears.

"Sure," he said. "And they're built for somebody running a 5k. All that soft stuff is made to spring you forward. You're not going forward. You're standing at the foot of my bed. So that soft stuff just squashes flat under you, and then you're standing on nothing."

He said he went through running shoes in a week on his route in his first year, until an old carrier set him straight.

Then he said three things I wrote down on a paper towel at the nurses' station, because nobody in 18 years had ever said them to me:

Your feet swell half a size over a long shift, so a narrow toe box squeezes tighter every hour.

A heavy shoe gets lifted thousands of times a night, so your legs pay for every ounce.

And shoes made to grip dry pavement aren't made for a floor somebody mopped ten minutes ago.

"You don't need a softer shoe," he said. "You need one built for standing."

When he was discharged two days later, he handed me the back of his breakfast menu. He'd written the name of the shoes his granddaughter wears. She's an ER nurse in Phoenix.

I didn't order them right away. I've been burned by "the one" too many times.

But I kept that menu card in my badge holder for a week. Then I ordered a pair.

**First shift:** honestly, I didn't notice anything. That was the strange part. Hour four came and went. Then hour eight. I walked to my car in my work shoes and forgot the slides in my locker.

**Week two:** three nights in a row. I was tired after the third, the normal kind. But the next morning my first step out of bed didn't feel like stepping on tacks.

**Week four:** I came home at 7:50, and instead of going to the couch, I made pancakes.

My daughter came downstairs, stopped in the kitchen doorway, and said:

"Is it a shift feet night?"

I said, "No shift feet."

She didn't say anything. She just got the syrup out.

**Month three:** my son had his first soccer game. I stood on the sideline the whole thing. Every time he looked over, I was standing there. After the game, he ran straight to me instead of his dad.

**Month six:** I picked up an extra shift without dreading it. Then another. Those two shifts paid for the shoes eight times over.

This year I'm working Christmas Eve again. Somebody has to.

But my mother-in-law is going to need a new picture.

Because Christmas morning, I'll be on the floor. In the wrapping paper. Putting batteries in something.
