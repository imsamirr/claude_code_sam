## PRIMARY TEXT

Day two of my trip to Florence, I was sitting on the wet stone steps of the Basilica of Santa Croce with my shoes off, fighting back tears in front of a tour group from Germany.

My twenty-year-old daughter Chloe was twenty feet away buying two paper cups of espresso. My left heel burned so hot I could not bear to let my foot touch the ground.

When Chloe walked back and saw me sitting barefoot on the cold, damp church steps, she stopped dead in her tracks.

What she said to me in that moment broke my heart more than the throbbing in my feet ever could.

I am fifty-eight years old. I have managed a busy dental office in Canton, Ohio, for twenty-two years.

Two years ago, my divorce was finalized after twenty-six years of marriage. It was a difficult chapter, but I promised myself I would start living again.

Chloe worked two summer jobs to spend her fall semester studying art history in Italy. I had saved every spare dollar for eighteen months to fly across the Atlantic to visit her. It was my very first time leaving the United States.

Chloe had planned nine days of walking for the two of us. She had every morning and afternoon mapped out in a little green notebook: the Uffizi Gallery, the Accademia, crossing the Ponte Vecchio, walking the Boboli Gardens, and climbing the steep stone hill up to San Miniato al Monte to watch the sunset over the terracotta roofs.

November in Florence is gorgeous, but the stone streets are cold, damp, and slick from autumn rain.

On our first full day, my phone logged 19,400 steps. By dinner time, my arches were aching, but I brushed it off. I told myself I was just tired from the flight.

By lunchtime on day two, after three hours on the uneven stones around the Piazza del Duomo, the bottom of my left foot felt like raw hamburger. Every step sent a sharp jolt through my heel and straight up into my calf.

My feet had swollen like sausages in the damp cold. The shoes that slipped on easily at breakfast in our small hotel felt two sizes too small by noon.

I was hobbling half a block behind Chloe, gritting my teeth so she would not see me grimacing.

Finally, my left heel gave out completely in front of Santa Croce. I could not take another step. I pulled my shoes off right there on the public steps just to stop the throbbing.

I had not been cheap about my footwear either. That was what made me so angry.

Before leaving Ohio, I bought a brand-new pair of $165 Hokas because everyone in my travel groups insisted they were the gold standard for walking. They felt soft and bouncy for about two hours in the airport.

But running shoes are built for straight-down impact on flat gym floors and rubber running tracks.

On uneven, rounded cobblestones, a thick squishy sole is a complete disaster. Every time my heel landed on an angled stone, the soft foam compressed and my ankle rolled side to side.

My stabilizing muscles were screaming from fighting to keep me upright on every single step.

Plus, at almost twice the weight of a normal shoe, lifting those heavy platforms twenty thousand times a day made my knees and lower back hurt worse than my feet.

Back home in Ohio, I already had a closet full of failed shoes.

I had a $140 pair of Brooks Ghost running shoes. They were designed to propel a runner forward on a track, with a narrow, tapered toe box that squeezed my toes together and pressed my bunion until I had worn a thin spot clean through the outer mesh.

I had a $180 pair of On Clouds that a coworker raved about. That rigid, triangle-shaped toe box was never built for real human feet, and by lunch hour my outer toes were completely numb.

I had a $75 pair of Skechers memory foam slip-ons that I wear at the office. That memory foam compresses under your weight and does not recover until you take them off. It gives you the least support right when you need it most, leaving you standing on flat, dead rubber after hour three.

I had even packed a $110 pair of cork Birkenstock clogs in my carry-on bag. The rigid cork footbed took my foot shape, but absorbed zero shock from hard stone, and being backless, my toes were clenching all day just trying to keep them on my feet.

I had spent over $650 on brand-name shoes. And here I was, stuck barefoot on church steps in Italy, watching other tourists walk by while my daughter stood there with two coffees.

That was when I learned why my feet were failing.

Your feet aren't wrecked from walking. They are wrecked from standing, and they are wrecked by shoes designed for the wrong job.

Running shoes are engineered for an athlete springing forward through a rapid stride. They are not built for a fifty-eight-year-old woman standing in museum lines, strolling down medieval alleys, or balancing on rounded flagstones.

Running foam is tuned to spring you through a stride, not hold you up standing still. That is why running foam packs flat by hour four under body weight.

And when your feet naturally swell up to half a size after hours on stone, a rigid running shoe turns into a vise.

Most shoes on the market are engineered for runners in a lab or athletes on television. They are not made for ordinary people whose feet have to survive real life.

They are built for a thirty-minute workout, not nine hours on hard stone.

I sat there in the drizzle with my head in my hands, holding my burning heel. The thought of ruining Chloe's week made me feel completely helpless. She had circled every museum and viewpoint in that notebook, and by day two her mother could not make it across a stone square. I felt embarrassed, exhausted, and ready to take a taxi back to the hotel and stay in bed until my flight home.

As I sat on those cold steps rubbing my aching arches, an older woman sitting two steps above me looked down and smiled.

Her name was Connie. She was about sixty, originally from Grand Rapids, Michigan. Connie had lived in Florence for seven years and worked as a professional walking tour guide, leading small groups through the historic center five days a week.

She looked at my bulky running shoes sitting on the stone next to me.

"First trip to Europe?" Connie asked gently.

I nodded, wiping my eyes.

"Running shoes," Connie said, pointing at them. "I see it every single week. People spend two hundred dollars on giant marshmallow sneakers, and by day two their ankles are shot and their heels are on fire. Cobblestones eat running shoes alive."

Connie stretched out her feet. She was wearing a pair of clean, low-profile black sneakers that looked sleek and normal, not clunky or orthopedic.

"Look up Comfort Wear," Connie told me. "The Ortho Stretch Cushion Shoe."

Connie said: "I walk twelve to fifteen miles every single week on medieval paving stones. In seven years of guiding tours, these are the only shoes that don't pack flat and don't let your feet roll."

"Comfortwear doesn't make shoes for athletes," Connie said. "They make shoes for people whose feet have to survive real life. Built for the shift, not the 5k. Not built for a runner, a lab, or a weekend."

I pulled out my phone right there on the church steps and looked them up.

They were on sale for $59.95. They were normally $90.

I had spent over six hundred dollars on brand-name running shoes that wrecked my feet on cobblestones, and the shoe that actually survived Florence was sixty bucks.

I asked Connie the exact objections buzzing in my head:

"Will I get my money back if they hurt my feet?"

Connie laughed. "They have over 500,000 customers. Comfort Wear gives you a plain 30-day money-back guarantee with a full refund within 30 days. No store credit games, no hassle. If they don't work on your feet, you get every penny back."

Then I asked: "Is it just a flimsy knit sock with a piece of rubber glued to the bottom?"

"Not even close," Connie said. "It is not a sock with a sole. The structural arch support is built right into the base of the shoe itself. It doesn't crush flat or pack out like a drugstore insert. And the sole is firm and stable, so when you step on an uneven cobblestone, your foot stays flat instead of wobbling side to side."

She explained that the upper is a breathable stretch-knit weave that gently gives as your feet swell throughout the day. The toe box is wide and anatomical, letting your toes spread out naturally without pinching bunions. The bottom has a slip-resistant rubber tread that grips slick wet marble. And they are featherlight, so your knees don't throb from lifting heavy platforms all day.

Connie wore a size 8.5, my exact shoe size. Her tour company office was just around the corner on Via de' Benci. She had a brand-new backup pair in her locker from the fall sale. She walked with Chloe and me to the office, handed me the fresh box, and insisted I try them on.

The first beat was honest:

They did not feel like squishy clouds, and there was no magical instant shock. What I felt was firm, steady support under my arch and an immediate sense of stability under my heel. My toes had room to breathe. The knit upper did not squeeze the sides of my swollen feet.

We walked back out onto the stone streets. After twenty minutes, the sharp burning in my heel began to settle down because the shoe held my foot steady instead of letting it twist on every stone.

By 5:00 PM, we had walked another four miles through the Oltrarno district.

For the first time on the trip, my feet did not throb. Not hurt less. Didn't hurt.

I went online that night at our hotel and ordered two pairs of my own for when I got home to Ohio.

On day eight, Chloe and I walked all the way up the steep stone switchbacks to the church of San Miniato al Monte, the highest point overlooking Florence.

We stood together at the marble railing, looking out over the entire city, the Duomo, the river Arno, and the red tiled roofs glowing golden in the sunset.

Other tourists were collapsed on stone benches around us, rubbing their ankles and groaning.

I was standing tall, smiling, with my arm around my daughter.

You're not the one sitting on a bench watching other people see what you flew there to see.

I turned to Chloe and reminded her of what she had said to me on the church steps of Santa Croce on day two.

When she found me barefoot and crying, she had knelt down, handed me the coffee, and said:

"Mom, you worked your whole life and saved for eighteen months to come see this city with me. If your feet can't walk, then we will just sit right here on these steps for the next seven days and watch the city together."

Standing on that terrace at San Miniato, I squeezed her hand and said:

"Chloe, we're not sitting on any steps today."
