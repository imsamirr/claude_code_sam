## PRIMARY TEXT

I packed $635 worth of "the best walking shoes" for our Disney trip.

On day four, I watched the fireworks from a bench, in my socks, with everybody's bags piled on my lap.

My daughter took a picture of the castle that night. You can see me in the corner of it. Tiny. Sitting down.

I'm writing this because if you're a bigger woman with a park trip coming up, the internet is going to tell you exactly what it told me. And it's wrong. Not a little wrong. Wrong in a way that costs you the trip.

Let me back up.

I'm Tanya. I'm 52. I wear a size 22, and I've been a plus-size woman my whole adult life, so I'm not new to my body. I know what it can do.

What I didn't know is what five days at Disney does to it.

This was the big one. My daughter, her husband, and my two grandkids. We'd been saving for two years. I was not going to be the reason anybody's day got cut short.

So I did my homework. I joined three Disney planning groups. I read every shoe thread. And I made a decision I was weirdly proud of at the time:

I'd bring the five most recommended shoes and wear a different pair every day. If one pair failed, I'd have a backup.

I basically ran my own shoe test. I just didn't know that's what I was doing.

Here's how it went.

**DAY 1: Skechers memory foam. $85.**

Everybody's first recommendation. "Like walking on a cloud."

And in the hotel lobby at 7 a.m., they were. I want to be fair about that. They felt amazing.

By 1 p.m. they felt like nothing.

Here's what nobody in those groups mentions: memory foam squashes under your weight and doesn't spring back until you take the shoes off. On a woman my size, that happens fast. By lunch the cushion was gone and I was basically standing on the insole. My arches started burning in the line for the first big ride.

The worst part is the timing. The shoe has the least support left right when you have the most day left.

**DAY 2: Hoka, the big ones. $165.**

The groups love these. "The only thing that saved my feet."

For two hours, honestly, I got it. All that cushion.

But here's how they fool you. That tall, soft sole is built to soak up impact going straight down, the way it lands when you're running on a flat road. A park day isn't that. It's ramps, curbs, cobblestone-style walkways, crowds you're dodging sideways. On uneven ground my foot rolled a little with every step, and all that foam did nothing to stop it.

And they're heavy. Almost twice the weight of a normal shoe. Lift that 20,000 times in a day and you feel it.

By evening it wasn't even my feet. It was my knees. I took the stairs at the hotel one at a time, holding the rail.

**DAY 3: Nike Air Max. $130.**

My son-in-law's suggestion. "They're comfortable and they look normal."

They are comfortable. At home. For a regular day.

But they're still a gym shoe, and the toe box is narrow. And here's the thing I learned on day three that I wish I'd known on day zero:

Your feet swell at Disney. Hours of heat, walking and standing will do that to anybody. On bigger women, it's more.

So the pair that fit perfectly at breakfast was squeezing me by lunch. By the parade my toes were jammed together like they were in a vise. I loosened the laces all the way down and it didn't matter. The shoe just didn't have any more room to give.

**DAY 4: On Cloud. $180.**

I bought these at the shopping district on night three, in a panic, because a woman in line told me they "changed her life."

Lightweight. Good-looking. Expensive.

And the toe box is shaped like a triangle. Not like a foot. Definitely not like a swollen foot on day four of a park trip.

By 2 p.m. I was looking for a bench.

By the fireworks I'd found one.

That's the picture. Me in the corner, shoes off, $635 of footwear back at the hotel, watching my grandkids watch the fireworks from fifty feet away.

My granddaughter, Bella, ran back to me halfway through and said, "Grandma, you can see better up front!"

I said, "I can see fine from here, baby."

I couldn't see anything. I was crying.

**DAY 5:** I didn't go. I told everybody I wanted a pool day.

I didn't want a pool day.

When we got home I did what I always do. I blamed my body.

I figured this is just what it costs to be my size at a theme park. The groups basically say so: pack ibuprofen, ice your feet at night, plan bench breaks.

I'd done all of it. It didn't matter.

Then, about a month later, someone posted something in one of those same Disney groups that stopped me cold.

A woman, a big girl like me, had asked hundreds of plus-size women who'd done Disney one question: *what shoes did you wear, and when did they quit?*

The answers were almost identical. Skechers flat by lunch. Hokas heavy, knees hurting. Nikes tight once the swelling started. On Clouds narrow by the afternoon.

It was my trip. Day by day. Hundreds of times over.

And then she wrote the thing that changed how I saw the whole week:

**"We're not walking at Disney. We're standing. And every one of those shoes was built for walking."**

I read it three times.

Because she was right. Think about an actual park day. You walk to a ride, then you stand in line for fifty minutes. Walk to lunch, stand in line. Walk to the parade, stand on the curb for an hour.

Running shoes are built to move you forward. That's what all that foam is for: to spring you into the next stride.

But when you're standing still, nothing's moving. Your whole weight just sits on that foam and presses it flat. Two or three hours of lines and it's gone. Your arch drops. Your heel takes every bit of the concrete. And your feet are swelling into a toe box that was never built to give.

That's why I failed five times with five different brands.

It was never five different problems. It was the same problem five times: I kept buying shoes made for runners, and I'm not a runner. I'm a woman standing in line on concrete for ten hours.

She listed what a park shoe actually needs. I wrote it on a sticky note and put it on my fridge:

1. Support that's built into the shoe, so there's no foam to pack flat by lunch.
2. A sole firm enough to protect you from concrete, but flexible enough that you're not clomping.
3. A wide toe box AND a top that stretches as your feet swell.
4. Light. You'll lift it 20,000 times.

Then, at the bottom of the post, the answer most of those women landed on.

It wasn't one of the big names. It wasn't the most expensive pair. It was a company I'd never heard of.

Comfort Wear. The Ortho Stretch Cushion Shoe.

About $60.

I laughed out loud, because I had literally just spent $180 on the pair that put me on the bench.

I was skeptical. I'd been burned four times in four days. But I checked it against my sticky note.

The arch support is built right into the shoe. There's no foam insert sitting in there waiting to collapse.

The sole is firm enough for concrete but still bends with your foot.

The toe box is wide, and the upper is a stretch knit. It gives as your feet swell instead of squeezing back.

And it's light. Lighter than any of the four I'd hauled around the parks.

Comfort Wear doesn't make shoes for athletes. They make them for people who stand for a living: nurses, teachers, people on their feet all day. Which, it turns out, is exactly what a park day is.

So I ordered a pair. Because we'd already booked the grandkids' spring trip, and I was not doing the bench again.

I wore them around home for a month first. My own little test.

I wore them on a full Saturday of errands, then at my granddaughter's dance recital, standing in the back for two hours.

Honestly, the first day I wasn't sure. They felt firmer than the clouds I was used to.

By the end of the second week I noticed something strange. I'd come home and forget to take them off.

Then came spring break.

**Day one:** 22,000 steps on my daughter's watch. My feet were tired. Normal tired. Good-day tired.

**Day two:** I put them on at the hotel and braced for the squeeze. It never came. My feet had swelled, like feet do, and the knit just moved with them.

**Day three:** the line for the big mountain ride was seventy minutes. In the sun. On concrete. I stood the whole thing holding Bella on my hip for the last ten minutes, because she was tired. Not me.

**Day four:** the day I'd been dreading.

My son-in-law sat down on a bench around three o'clock and said, "You guys go ahead, my feet are done."

He was wearing Hokas.

**Day five:** I went to the parks. No pool day.

On the last night, at the fireworks, Bella grabbed my hand and pulled.

"Grandma! Up front!"

And I went up front.

Here's what I want to be clear about. My feet didn't hurt less.

They didn't hurt.

There's a new castle picture on my fridge. I'm not in the corner of it. I'm in the middle, with a grandbaby on each side, and I'm standing up.
