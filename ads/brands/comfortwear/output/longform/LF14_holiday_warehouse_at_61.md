## PRIMARY TEXT

By three in the morning on my third night at the Groveport fulfillment center, two twenty-two-year-old boys started quietly carrying my yellow tote bins for me so my scanner wouldn't flash red.

I was sixty-one years old, standing on six inches of sealed industrial concrete, and I was crying behind my safety glasses because my heels hurt so bad I couldn't lift my feet off the floor.

I took that seasonal night shift last November because my billing clerk job of fourteen years disappeared that spring.

My severance had run out in August.

I have three grandkids: Liam, who was seven, Sadie, who was four, and baby Nora.

Their mother was working two jobs as a dental assistant, and their father had walked out two years earlier.

I had promised myself that Christmas morning wasn't going to be empty for those babies just because their grandmother lost her office desk.

So I put on my coat, signed up for the peak holiday rush, and took the night shift from six in the evening to four-thirty in the morning.

Ten hours a night.

Four nights a week.

Fifteen to twenty thousand steps across bare concrete under stadium lights.

Nobody tells you what sealed industrial concrete does to an older woman's body until you stand on it.

Concrete has zero give.

Every step drives every pound of your body weight straight back up through your heel bone, your plantar fascia, and into your lower back.

By hour four, walking feels like grinding broken glass into your heels.

By hour seven, you're not walking. You're shuffling.

The two young men on my packing lane, Tyler and Devon, noticed it on my third shift.

They were young enough to be my grandsons, wearing backwards baseball caps, practically jogging between the conveyor belts.

I was gripping the edge of the metal packing table just to stay upright while taping cardboard boxes.

My scanner was beeping every forty seconds, counting down units per hour.

When I leaned down to pick up a heavy tote bin full of blenders, my left heel gave a sharp, sickening throb.

My knees buckled.

I had to grab the steel shelving rack with both hands to keep from dropping to the concrete.

Tyler saw it.

He walked over without saying a word, lifted the forty-pound tote onto my table, and scanned the barcode for me.

Ten minutes later, Devon carried my next tote.

They were trying to protect an older woman from getting called out for falling behind quota.

And it broke my heart.

I had worked my entire life.

I had raised two children on my own.

Now I was sixty-one years old, being carried through a warehouse shift by two kids who felt sorry for me because my feet were broken down.

At four in the morning, the shift supervisor, Danica, waved me into the break room office.

My stomach dropped.

I thought I was getting let go right then and there.

Danica closed the door and looked at my pale face.

"Linda," she said. "You're dragging your left leg. The cameras pick it up, and if safety stops you, I have to send you home. What are you wearing on your feet?"

I looked down at the $25 discount department store sneakers I had bought for the job.

The cardboard insoles had pancaked completely flat by lunchtime on my very first shift.

I was basically standing barefoot on concrete.

I told Danica I couldn't afford to lose this job.

I told her about Liam, Sadie, and Nora.

Danica took a breath and pointed toward the lockers.

"Talk to Wanda on Line 4 before you clock out," Danica said. "She's sixty-two. She's worked peak for five seasons. If she can survive sixty hours a week on this concrete, you can too. But you have to fix what's on your feet."

That morning, before I talked to Wanda, I tried what everyone online recommends.

I fell into the exact same expensive trap that ruins thousands of working women.

I had the exact same graveyard in my bedroom: $180 for Hokas, $200 for Danskos, $150 for Brooks, all sitting useless in my closet."

I went to the store in Columbus that afternoon, desperate for anything that would let me keep working.

First was Skechers memory foam for $75.

They compressed completely under my weight by hour two and didn't recover until I pulled them off in my car, giving least support right when my heels needed it most.

Next was Brooks Ghost for $140.

Built to move a runner forward on a track, the narrow tapered toe box pressed my bunion raw, until a hole was worn clean through at the bunion by the third night.

Then came Dansko clogs for $160.

A rigid footbed that was rock hard from day one, like strapping heavy wooden paving blocks to my feet.

Lifting that heavy rigid sole for eighteen thousand steps a night made my knees hurt more than my feet.

I tried Birkenstock sandals for $110 around the house on my day off.

The cork took my foot shape, but absorbed none of the impact, and backless meant toes clenched all day trying to keep them on.

Then Vionic shoes for $130.

Rigid mold, one fixed shape, and feet don't hold one shape during a ten-hour shift. They swell.

By 1:00 AM, that rigid plastic ridge was cutting into my swollen arch like a steel ruler.

I tried Nike Air Max for $130.

Built for running tracks and gym floors, the air bubble was there for the look, not my arch.

I tried On Cloud sneakers for $180.

Triangle-shaped toe box, not made for your feet, real tight by lunch.

My most expensive mistake was Hokas for $175.

Felt great for 2 hours on the carpet because the foam was so thick and plush.

Then I wore them on the concrete floor at the warehouse.

The foam was built for straight-down impact, so on uneven concrete and ramps, my foot rolled side to side.

They were almost twice the weight of normal shoes, knees hurting more than feet, and unstable when crouching down to scan bottom bins.

I had spent over nine hundred dollars of money I didn't have, putting it on a credit card I took the warehouse job to pay down.

And my feet were still screaming.

By the end of my second week, I was ready to quit.

On Friday morning, I ran into Wanda by the metal lockers.

Wanda is sixty-two, with silver hair pulled back in a hairnet, looking fresh after a ten-hour shift.

She saw me sitting on the metal bench, gripping my calf, fighting back tears.

"Linda," Wanda said, sitting down next to me. "Danica told me you were struggling. Look down at my feet."

She was wearing clean, stylish black sneakers with white soles.

"I work sixty hours a week during peak season," Wanda said. "I walk twelve miles a night on this concrete. If I wore running shoes or clogs, I'd be flat on my back on the couch by Thanksgiving. Running shoes are made for a runner in a 5k, not a sixty-year-old woman standing ten hours on concrete."

Wanda pulled a scrap of paper from her pocket and wrote down five words.

Comfort Wear Ortho Stretch Cushion Shoe.

"Look them up today," Wanda said.

I looked at the scrap of paper and hesitated.

"Is this from an online ad?" I asked.

I know better than to buy shoes off internet ads.

Half of them are fly-by-night companies that ship cheap plastic trash from a shipping container four weeks later with zero customer service.

Wanda laughed.

"I thought the exact same thing," Wanda said. "I don't trust internet ads either. But Comfort Wear is a real company with 500,000+ customers. They give you a 30-day money-back guarantee with a full refund within 30 days if your feet don't agree. Podiatrists recommend them, doctors recommend them, and they are HSA and FSA eligible."

Wanda pulled her shoe off and handed it to me.

"Feel how this is built," she said.

It was not a flimsy knit sock with a rubber sole glued on.

Cheap knit look-alikes fall apart in two months and give zero support.

The stretch-knit upper was flexible, with structural arch support built directly into the base of the shoe, not an insert that packs flat.

The arch bridge stayed firm without collapsing under body weight.

The sole was firm-but-flexible and stable, not squishy like wobbly Hoka foam.

The wide toe box gave toes room to spread without pinching bunions, and it was light compared to heavy Danskos.

"How much are they?" I asked.

Wanda told me they were normally $90, but on sale right now for $59.95: about $60.

A $60 shoe was doing what my $175 Hokas and $140 Brooks never could.

Sixty dollars was holding up to ten hours of warehouse concrete better than nearly a thousand dollars of brand-name running shoes.

I ordered a pair in black that morning.

The box was waiting on my front steps when I got home from my Sunday afternoon sleep.

I took the shoes out of the tissue paper and looked at them.

No flashy gimmicks, no thick clown soles.

Just clean, solid shoes built for people who have to earn a living on hard floors.

Built for the ten-hour shift, not the 5k track.

Here was my honest first beat:

Monday night, I laced them up for my shift.

Walking from the gravel parking lot into the security turnstiles, my heel didn't send that sharp wincing stab up my leg.

Ten hours on industrial concrete is still hard physical labor.

By four-thirty in the morning, my muscles were exhausted, but the burning fire in my heels was gone.

The structural arch had held its ground all night without pancaking flat.

By hour seven, ten hours on concrete will puff your feet up like bread dough.

In my old sneakers, the laces felt like wire cutting into the tops of my feet.

With these, the stretch-knit upper gave naturally with my foot, without squeezing my bunion.

I walked out past Tyler and Devon, carrying my own totes.

It got better shift by shift as my feet adapted to real support.

By the middle of my second week, my plantar fascia had settled down because the arch wasn't collapsing under my weight.

By week three, when the four-thirty horn blew, I walked through the security gates, out into the cold dawn air, and across the asphalt to my sedan with a normal stride.

I unlocked the door, sat down, and realized something that made me smile:

My heels were not throbbing.

Not hurt less. Didn't hurt.

I didn't have to unlace my sneakers in the front seat just to drive home without crying.

I finished that entire peak season at Groveport.

Four weeks of ten-hour shifts on sealed industrial concrete, and the structural arch support never packed flat.

The slip-resistant sole gripped slick lanes, and the knit upper didn't tear.

On my last night before Christmas Eve, Danica handed me my pay envelope with my overtime bonus.

Tyler and Devon walked up before the shift ended and handed me a card signed by the whole crew with chocolates.

They weren't carrying my boxes anymore.

They treated me like an equal who had stood shoulder to shoulder with them through the hardest rush of the year.

The next morning, I pushed a full cart down the toy aisle: building blocks for Liam, a dollhouse for Sadie, and a musical bear for baby Nora.

Every gift paid for in full, with money I earned on my own two feet.

Peak season hiring is starting back up at Groveport, and yesterday I signed my contract for a second holiday run.

This time, I'm not dreading the concrete.
