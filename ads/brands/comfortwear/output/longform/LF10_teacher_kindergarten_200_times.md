## PRIMARY TEXT

Two hundred and fourteen times.

That was the exact number of times I went down to the classroom floor and back up before the 3:15 PM dismissal bell rang.

I know because on a chilly Tuesday in November, I kept tally marks on my clipboard.

I have taught kindergarten for twenty-six years in Fort Worth, Texas. In kindergarten, you live on the floor.

You sit cross-legged on the alphabet rug for morning circle. You crouch down to tie double-knotted sneakers after recess. You drop to one knee to zip a stubborn winter coat.

You crouch to eye level so a crying child feels safe. You crawl between plastic chairs to fish out runaway crayons. You kneel on hard linoleum to wipe spilled apple juice.

For twenty-six years, I never counted. I just did it.

Then came a Tuesday morning during reading circle.

I was sitting on the carpet with twenty-two children, reading a story. When it ended, I put my hands on both knees, braced my body weight, and pushed myself up to stand.

As I stood, my knees popped and an involuntary groan came out of my chest. It sounded like an old screen door in a windstorm.

Toby, a five-year-old sitting right in front of me, looked up with wide, worried eyes.

"Mrs. Miller," he whispered. "Why do you make that hurting sound every time you stand up?"

The room went quiet. Toby was not trying to be disrespectful. He was five. He was genuinely worried his teacher was breaking apart.

I laughed it off and told him my knees were just waking up. But inside, I felt a wave of cold shame wash over my neck.

He was right. I was making that sound every single time I stood up from the floor.

I was groaning because my plantar fascia felt like it was tearing off my heel bones. My lower back was throbbing from the torque of standing up without ankle support, and my knees felt like they were packed with coarse gravel.

That was the morning I grabbed a black ballpoint pen and decided to count.

Every time my knees bent down to the floor, I drew a tally mark on my clipboard.

Tying shoelaces: 42 times.
Zipping coats and jackets: 28 times.
Carpet circle lessons: 14 times.
Comforting crying children at eye level: 19 times.
Picking up stray crayons and scissors: 51 times.
Recess duty crouching on cracked blacktop: 24 times.
Wiping spills and checking cubbies: 36 times.

Total: 214 times in seven hours. Down to the concrete under that thin commercial carpet, and back up again.

Doing two hundred and fourteen squats a day on slab concrete will wreck your body if your footwear fails you.

Over the last five years, out of sheer desperation to survive to the final bell without limping to my car, I tested virtually every shoe recommended in the teacher lounge.

Here is my Teacher Footwear Scoreboard:

Dansko Professional Clogs ($160). Rock hard footbed from day one with zero shock absorption. Worse, the thick wooden-feel platform has zero flex at the ball of your foot. When you crouch to tie a shoe, the rigid sole levers your heel straight out, twisting your ankle and forcing your knees to take the brunt. Grade: D.

Hoka Bondi 8 ($175). That massive 40mm stack of marshmallow foam felt plush for straight walking. But crouch down to zip a coat, and that squishy foam tilts and rolls from side to side like balancing on a waterbed. In a crouch, you lose your balance and have to grab a table just to avoid falling over. Grade: C-.

Brooks Ghost ($140). Engineered to push a runner forward on a track. The narrow tapered toe box pinched the bunion on my left foot until it throbbed by morning recess. Within four months, my bunion wore a hole clean through the mesh. Grade: D+.

On Cloud ($180). Sleek and trendy, but the triangle-shaped toe box crammed my forefoot into a tight wedge. By lunch duty, my toes were screaming and numb. Grade: D.

Skechers Memory Foam ($80). Soft for thirty minutes. But memory foam compresses under body weight and does not recover until you take the shoes off. By 10:30 AM, it packed flat as cardboard, offering zero arch support right when I needed it most. Grade: F.

Nike Air Max ($130). That air bubble is there for looks, not your arch. It left the middle of my foot completely hollow, and my heels took the full shock during recess duty on blacktop. Grade: D-.

Vionic Walkers ($130). Rigid orthopedic footbed that holds one fixed shape. But teacher feet swell up to half a size. By 2:00 PM, the rigid sidewalls choked my instep until my feet felt like sausages in a casing. Grade: C.

Birkenstock Clogs ($110). The cork footbed molded to my foot, but offered zero impact protection on school linoleum. Because they were backless, my toes clenched with every step just to keep them on, slapping the floor and tiring out my shins. Grade: D.

Every pair failed the same test: they were built for walking, not crouching and planting.

Here is what nobody in the athletic shoe industry understands about teachers: you are not walking five miles in a straight line. You are planted.

When you crouch down to child level, the ball of your foot bends up to ninety degrees. Your entire body weight transfers onto your forefoot, while your heel lifts.

If your shoe is rock hard, it levers your heel out. If your shoe is squishy, it wobbles side to side.

And if you teach with strict dress codes, administration rules create another hurdle. At our school, the principal banned athletic sneakers with neon logos. Many teachers said: "I can't wear sneakers to work."

I was stuck between agonizing foot pain and breaking the district dress code.

Then came a Friday afternoon during bus duty.

Karen, a fifty-eight-year-old first-grade teacher who has been in the classroom for thirty-one years, walked up beside me on the blacktop.

She was standing tall, relaxed, chatting with parents, while I was shifting my weight back and forth like a flamingo trying to give my heels a break.

"Beth," Karen said softly. "You look like you are about to cry."

I winced. "Karen, I went down to the floor over two hundred times today. My arches are on fire, my knees feel like glass, and the principal just reminded us that business-casual shoes are required on Monday."

Karen smiled, stepped forward, and pointed down at her feet.

She was wearing a pair of sleek, clean, all-black shoes. They did not have giant neon logos. They did not have massive white marshmallow soles. They looked like tailored, professional black flats with a clean modern finish.

"These are not running shoes," Karen said. "And they are not clunky orthopedic boots. They are Comfort Wear Ortho Stretch Cushion Shoes."

I looked at them skeptically. "Karen, they look like regular sneakers. My feet are wrecked. I need heavy medical support, not cute shoes off the internet."

Karen laughed. "Beth, I have bone spurs on both heels and a bunion the size of a golf ball. These cost sixty dollars. And they scored higher than my one hundred seventy-five dollar Hokas on day one."

A sixty-dollar shoe outscoring the most expensive pairs on my board?

I wrote the name down, but I was skeptical. I know Facebook is flooded with cheap dropshipped shoes that arrive as flimsy foam slippers with zero support.

When I got home, I sat with an ice pack on my arches and researched Comfort Wear.

I ordered two pairs: one in all-black for school and one in navy for weekends.

When the box arrived, I slipped them on.

My honest first impression was comfort, not magic. My knees did not magically turn twenty again.

The real test was Monday morning.

At 8:30 AM, I sat cross-legged on the carpet circle for phonics. My feet felt supported without pressure points.

During recess, I crouched down to tie untied sneakers on the blacktop. My heel did not lever out of the shoe, and my ankles did not wobble. The flexible sole bent smoothly with the ball of my foot, keeping me planted.

At 1:30 PM, when afternoon swelling set in, the stretch knit upper expanded gently around my bunion instead of pinching.

Then came the true payoff.

The bell rang. I closed the book, stood straight up from the floor in one smooth motion, and walked toward the cubbies.

I did not put my hands on my knees. I did not groan. I did not have to grab the bookshelf to pull myself upright.

Toby looked up from the rug, his face breaking into a huge smile.

"Mrs. Miller! Your knees didn't make the screen door sound!"

I laughed so hard tears came to my eyes.

For the first time in ten years, I made it to the 3:15 PM bell with energy left in my body. I did not limp to my car. I had energy to grade papers, cook dinner, and enjoy my evening.
