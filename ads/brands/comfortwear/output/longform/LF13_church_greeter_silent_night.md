## PRIMARY TEXT

I slipped into the very back row during the opening chord of "Silent Night" so nobody in the sanctuary would see me sit down.

For thirty-one years, I have been the Sunday greeter at the front double doors of our little church in Columbia, Tennessee.

I know every family that walks through those wooden doors.

I know who lost a spouse that spring, who just had a baby, and who needs the large-print bulletin before they even ask for it.

For thirty-one years, I stood at those doors welcoming people in out of the cold.

Last Christmas Eve, I couldn't make it to the end of the service.

The candlelight service was always my favorite hour of the entire year.

The sanctuary lights go down low.

The brass bells ring out from the choir loft.

Everyone holds a white wax candle with a paper drip cup around the base.

From the time I was eight years old, holding my mother's hand in that church, I stood for all three verses of "Silent Night."

Sixty years in a row.

I stood when I was pregnant with my oldest boy.

I stood with a broken wrist in a cast back in 1989.

I stood the December after my husband passed, even though my eyes were so full of tears I couldn't read the hymnal.

I always stood.

Last December twenty-fourth, my heels were on fire.

It felt like someone had driven two red-hot roofing nails straight up through the centers of both heel bones.

My doctor called it plantar fasciitis six months earlier.

He gave me a paper handout with calf stretches and told me to roll a frozen water bottle under my arch every evening.

I rolled that frozen bottle until my toes turned numb, but the throbbing came right back twenty minutes later.

By December, the first two steps out of bed every morning were pure misery.

I would sit on the edge of the mattress, grip the nightstand with both hands, and brace myself before my feet touched the floorboards.

I had to hobble to the kitchen like a ninety-year-old woman just to start the coffee pot.

At church, the front foyer has hard terrazzo tile laid over concrete.

Standing on that floor for two hours before the service was breaking me down.

So when the organist started the introduction to "Silent Night," my body gave out.

My left heel throbbed so hard my knee began to shake.

I knew if I stayed on my feet another two minutes, I was going to lose my balance in front of the congregation.

I backed into the dark corner of the rear pew, sat down on the oak bench, pulled my coat over my lap, and blew my candle out.

I sat there in the dark while four hundred people stood with their lights held high, singing.

I felt humiliated.

I thought nobody noticed.

I was wrong.

Someone standing four pews ahead turned around in the candlelight, looked right at me, and saw the whole thing.

Her name is Rachel.

Rachel is thirty-four, and she works as an emergency room nurse over at the hospital in town.

She didn't say a word that night.

She just let me sit there with my candle out.

Over the next month, I went on an expensive hunt for shoes.

I was ready to spend whatever it took to stop that burning.

Like a lot of women my age, I ended up building an expensive graveyard of failed shoes in the bottom of my closet.

I had the exact same graveyard sitting in my bedroom: $180 for Hokas, $200 for Danskos, $150 for Brooks.

All of them sitting in a row on the closet floor, completely useless.

First was a pair of Skechers memory foam for $75.

The girl at the store said they were like walking on pillows.

They compressed under my weight and didn't recover until I took them off, giving me the least support right when you need it most.

Next was Brooks Ghost for $140.

They are built to move a runner forward, with a narrow tapered toe box that presses a bunion exactly where it hurts, until a hole was worn clean through at the bunion.

Then came Dansko clogs for $160.

They had a rigid footbed that was rock hard from day one, and they were the heaviest shoes I have ever lifted.

Lifting that heavy rigid sole made my knees hurt more than my feet.

I tried Birkenstock cork sandals for $110 around the house.

The cork took my foot shape and none of its impact, and because they are backless, toes clench all day trying to keep them on.

Then I bought Vionic shoes for $130.

They had a rigid mold with one fixed shape, and feet don't hold one shape all morning.

They swell.

By noon, that hard plastic ridge was digging straight into my arch.

I tried Nike Air Max for $130.

They were built for running tracks and gym floors, and the air bubble is there for the look, not your arch.

I tried On Cloud sneakers for $180.

They had a triangle-shaped toe box, not made for your feet, real tight by lunch.

My most expensive mistake was a pair of Hokas for $165.

They felt great for 2 hours on my living room carpet.

Then I wore them on a rainy Sunday at church.

The foam was built for straight-down impact, so on uneven ground your foot rolls side to side.

When I stepped on the uneven brick walk outside the church doors, my foot rolled outward.

I caught myself on the handrail, but my heart was racing.

At sixty-eight years old, falling is the one thing you cannot risk.

Seniors are falling in high-stack soft shoes because you have zero ground feel.

On top of that, they were almost twice the weight, so knees hurt more than feet, and they were unstable when you crouch down to pick up a dropped hymnal.

I was over eight hundred dollars in the hole, and I still couldn't stand for twenty minutes without burning pain.

By late January, I was ready to resign from the greeting committee after thirty-one years.

That Sunday in the fellowship hall, Rachel walked up while I was pouring decaf coffee.

She looked down at the beige flats I was wearing, then looked me straight in the eyes.

"Miss Martha," she said. "I saw you blow your candle out on Christmas Eve."

I looked down at my coffee cup and admitted it.

I told her my heels were burning so bad I couldn't bear my own weight, and I told her about the eight pairs of shoes sitting in my closet.

Rachel nodded.

She looked down at her own feet.

She wore a pair of clean black sneakers with white soles that looked like normal, stylish walking shoes.

"Miss Martha, I do twelve-hour shifts on sealed hospital concrete," Rachel said.

"Every new nurse shows up in Hokas or Danskos. By month two, their arches are wrecked because running shoes are made for a runner on a track, not someone standing on hard floors all day. The only shoe that survived my shifts is this one."

She pulled a pen from her purse and wrote the name on a paper napkin.

Comfort Wear Ortho Stretch Cushion Shoe.

"Look them up when you get home," Rachel said.

I looked at the napkin and hesitated.

"Is that from a Facebook ad?" I asked.

I'm sixty-eight, and I know better than to buy shoes off Facebook ads.

Half of them are fly-by-night companies that ship cheap knockoffs weeks later and vanish.

My neighbor ordered knit shoes off an ad last year: a flimsy knit sock glued to thin rubber.

Zero support, not for two-hour shifts let alone twelve hours.

Rachel laughed.

"I had the exact same worry," Rachel said. "I don't trust internet ads either. But Comfort Wear is a real company with 500,000+ customers. They give you a 30-day money-back guarantee with a full refund within 30 days if your feet don't love them. Podiatrists recommend them, doctors recommend them, and they are HSA and FSA eligible."

Rachel slipped her shoe off and handed it to me right there by the coat rack.

"Feel how this is built," she said.

It was not a flimsy sock with a sole.

The stretch-knit upper was flexible, but the foundation was completely solid.

Built directly inside the shoe was structural arch support, molded right into the base so there was no foam insert to pack flat or slide around.

The arch held steady without caving in.

The sole was firm-but-flexible and stable, steady under your foot, not squishy like wobbly Hoka foam.

The toe box was wide enough for toes to spread out naturally, and it weighed almost nothing compared to heavy Danskos.

"How much are they?" I asked.

Rachel told me they were normally $90, but on sale for $59.95: about $60.

A $60 shoe was doing what my $165 Hokas and $140 Brooks never could.

Sixty dollars was fixing what nearly a thousand dollars in running shoes and clogs couldn't touch.

I ordered a pair in black that afternoon.

When the box arrived on my porch that Thursday, I opened it right on the kitchen table.

I didn't expect a miracle.

I'm sixty-eight years old, and I have lived long enough to know shoes don't work magic overnight.

Comfort Wear makes shoes for people whose feet have to survive real life.

Built for the shift, not the 5k.

Not built for a runner, a lab, or a weekend jogger.

Here was my honest first beat:

Tuesday morning, I slipped them on to walk down my gravel driveway for the paper.

My feet were still tender from months of inflammation, but for the first time, my heel didn't feel that sickening stab on the stones.

The structural arch met my foot and held it firm, the stable sole absorbed the gravel without wobbling, and the wide toe box left zero pressure on my bunion.

The real test came on Sunday.

I stood at the double front doors for two hours, greeting eighty-two people.

The slip-resistant grip held the slick terrazzo floor firmly, without my ankles rolling once.

By late morning, after three hours on the terrazzo tile, my feet did what older feet always do on hard surfaces: they expanded.

Normal shoes start biting into your arches like tight bands.

With these, the stretch-knit upper gave with my foot, without squeezing my bunion.

When the service ended, my legs were tired from standing, but that sharp burning fire in my heels had quieted down to a dull murmur.

For the first time in a year, I walked down the brick steps and across the church lot to my Buick without having to limp or kick my shoes off before turning the ignition key.

It got better Sunday by Sunday as my feet adjusted to having real structural support under them.

By my third week, the difference was unmistakable.

When the organ began the closing hymn, I walked to my pew and reached to lean on the back out of habit.

And then I stopped.

My heels were not throbbing.

Not hurt less. Didn't hurt.

I stood through the entire service, all four hymns, and the benediction on two steady feet.

That was ten months ago.

I have worn my Comfort Wear shoes every Sunday since.

The structural arch support has not packed flat.

And in a few weeks, Christmas Eve will be here again.

For three years, I dreaded that night.

I dreaded the burning in my heels, and I dreaded having to blow my candle out while everyone else sang.

This year is going to be different.

When the sanctuary lights go down low and the brass bells ring out from the loft, I won't be looking for an empty bench.

I will be standing by the choir rail, holding my white candle high.

Rachel will be standing four pews away, and when the organist strikes the opening chords of "Silent Night," I will be standing on steady feet, singing every single word of all three verses from memory.

If you have spent hundreds on running shoes, clogs, and fancy foam that left your heels burning, stop wasting your money.

Your feet aren't wrecked from walking.

They're wrecked from standing.
