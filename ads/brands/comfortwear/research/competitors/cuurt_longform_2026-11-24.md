## 2. The Cuurt system: what each ad does

### 2.1 The narrator is the *buyer*, not the sufferer
A daughter, wife, coach or neighbor watches **a man 60+** decline. The reader is the wife or daughter who buys for him, and the offer is built for that: "buy one, get one free… **one for him and one for you**." The reader sees herself in the narrator and her husband or dad in the sufferer.

### 2.2 The symptom is a personality change, not foot pain
The opening shows **behavior**, never feet:
- sitting in the car for 30–40 minutes
- skipping coffee after games
- snapping at his wife
- throwing a paddle
- walking off at 9–9
- going from 5 mornings a week to 2

The foot pain is the **reveal** at about 35–45% ("both feet in a bucket of ice water… a half-empty bottle of Advil"). This turns a boring problem (sore feet) into a mystery about someone you love ("what's wrong with Dad?"), and the answer, "his shoes", is a relief: fixable and not his age.

### 2.3 The hook formula
> **I [did an ordinary thing for an unrelated reason]. Instead it showed me [a loved one's hidden behavior]. It took [a dramatic event], [a consequence] and [an unexpected messenger] to find out why.**
>
> "I put a security camera on my parents' driveway to catch a package thief. Instead it showed me that my dad, Gary, 66, was sitting in his car for 30 to 40 minutes after every pickleball game before he came inside. It took a thrown paddle, a suspension from his pickleball club and a 74-year-old man to find out why."

- The three-item teaser ends on the most unexpected one (a 74-year-old man).
- Exact numbers and names come in sentence 2 (Gary, 66, 30–40 minutes).
- Other cold opens: a stranger's wallet in a cart, a misdelivered package, the coach's notebook, "I almost called the police".

### 2.4 An evidence device that later becomes the results log
Each story has a **recording instrument**: the security camera, the coach's notebook, the court-booking app, a paper tracing, the X-ray. It **proves the decline** (bookings went 5 → 3 → 2) and later **proves the recovery**, in observed behavior rather than pain scores:
- "Week 1, he still sat in the car, but for nine minutes instead of thirty."
- "Week 2, the garage light stopped coming on at night."
- "Week 4… No stop at the mailbox."
- "Week 6, he stayed for coffee."

Results told through behavior read as *proof*, not as a claim.

### 2.5 A free at-home test the reader can do tonight
**The single strongest device.** Every ad gives a 2-minute, free self-diagnosis:
- "Take the insole out of his court shoe, put it on the floor and have him stand on it barefoot. If his toes hang off the sides, the shoe is the wrong shape."
- Or: "Trace his foot on printer paper and put his shoe on top."

**Why it works:**
- It's a micro-commitment that **proves the mechanism to the reader with her own eyes**: the belief is installed by her, not by the ad.
- It works as a CTA for people who aren't ready to buy ("do the test tonight").
- It sets the **buying criteria** that only their product passes.
- It's repeated in the body, the close and the P.S.

### 2.6 A three-check mechanism anyone can verify with their hands
> 1. **Stand on the insole:** every toe should fit on it.
> 2. **Hold the shoe sideways:** the heel shouldn't be higher than the toes.
> 3. **Press the sole with your thumb:** it should be firm, not soft.

Each check maps to a symptom:
- **Pointed front:** toes can't spread or grip, so the calves overwork ("rock hard by game three").
- **Raised heel:** tips him forward, causing morning heel pain ("stepping on a nail").
- **Soft foam:** he sinks and wobbles, so ankles, knees and hips overwork.

**The checks are the bridge.** "We spent a week checking every court shoe… One passed all three: CUURT." The brand arrives at about **70–78%**, as the survivor of the criteria.

### 2.7 Every rival fix is defeated in advance
- **Orthotics, Advil, ice:** "take the edge off… don't change the shape of the shoe." The analogy: "Advil is like taking the battery out of a smoke alarm."
- **The cheap copy:** "a $59 'wide toe box' pickleball shoe on Amazon… After six weeks nothing had changed… the same pointed shoe, just a little wider." This sits in every ad and kills the "I'll buy a cheaper wide one" objection.
- **Bigger sizes, gel, inserts:** Frank's shelf of **eleven pairs** of court shoes.
- **The doctor:** "X-rays looked fine for his age… nobody ever asked about his shoes." It isn't anti-doctor. The doctor reverses at the end: "Whatever you're doing, keep doing it" (in all 4 ads read).

### 2.8 The mirror story (the cautionary twin)
The wallet ad uses **two husbands**. Walt never got the fix: he declined, fell, broke his hip and went to assisted living. Ray, the narrator's husband, did get the fix and came back. The stranger's line, "They treated his feet for three years. **Nobody ever asked about his shoes**", is the thesis, said by a third party. The package ad does the same with neighbor Hank. It creates fear without the narrator having to threaten anyone.

### 2.9 The payoff is social repair, not just no pain
- He apologizes, and partners with the man he threw the paddle at.
- He brings the donuts again.
- "I have my husband back."
- The person he wronged becomes his partner.

The emotional payoff is **getting the person back**. The pain relief is almost secondary.

### 2.10 Offer and close
- **BOGO**, framed for gifting: "one for him and one for you."
- The risk reversal is a **100-day free size swap**, not a money-back guarantee. It targets the real fear in buying shoes, which is the size.
- **Stacked P.S. lines** (up to 5): test it tonight, warning signs to watch for, "check every shoe against the tracing, *including CUURT*" (a credibility move), the swap, a light callback ("he brings the donuts on Fridays").
- **The link goes straight to the product page.** No advertorial, CTA "Learn more".

### 2.11 Line craft
- Grade 4–6, with one short beat per line.
- Blue-collar backstories make the decline land: a union electrician for 31 years, a roofer for 38, a firefighter for 30, a mail carrier for 34. "He never complained."
- Retiree hubs make it local and real: The Villages, Ocala, Sarasota, Wildwood, Publix, Oak Run, Buena Vista.
- Exact numbers everywhere: 32 and 41 minutes, $400 with a rubber band, 9–9, five mornings.
- Small domestic objects carry the emotion: the dusty paddle in the rack, one mug on the drying rack, court shoes on the empty chair.

### 2.12 Images
One phone-real photo of the hook moment, square 600×600:
- a **night-vision security-camera frame with a timestamp** (11:08 PM)
- the wallet with its rubber band in a red cart
- the paddle smash at the net
- a close-up of the bump on an older foot

The images are literal: the image *is* the first sentence. The camera frame breaks our "no fake UI" rule on purpose, because the story's device *is* a camera.

### 2.13 Authority variants (rank lower so far)
These are 17–34k characters long:
- a revision surgeon learning from a 74-year-old Villages doctor
- a surgeon from a Greek fishing village where barefoot old men have no bunions
- an orthodontist husband

They use "two problems, two answers" mechanism framing and cite *classes* of studies (barefoot populations, raised-heel load), with careful hedges ("nobody is claiming the bone goes back"). They're useful as a second lane, but the witness stories are winning.

## 3. What this means for Comfort Wear (what to borrow and what to avoid)

**Borrow (formats and mechanics only, never their lines or story details):**
1. **Witness-buyer narrator,** for Christmas gifting: a daughter or husband watching **mom** decline (Comfort Wear is a women's shoe). Close with "one for her and one for you" when the live offer allows it. For Christmas, use the brand.json Christmas offer.
2. **Behavior-first mystery:** she leaves the grandkids' game early, skips the walk after church, sits in the car, snaps, cancels the trip. Reveal the feet at about 40%.
3. **An evidence device that becomes the results log:** a step counter or watch app, a family group-chat photo history, a Ring doorbell, a calendar of walks. Results shown as behavior ("week 3, she walked the whole mall").
4. **A free at-home test tied to OUR mechanism.** Don't copy the insole-width test (we avoid "extra-wide" promises). Use ours:
   - **"The 3 p.m. squeeze":** shoes that fit at 7 a.m. and pinch by 3 p.m. don't give when feet swell. That's our stretch-knit upper.
   - **"The insole dent test":** pull the insole after a long day. A flat, dented heel means the foam has packed down and the arch is gone. That's our built-in arch versus foam.
   - **"The thumb test":** press the heel of the insole. If it stays dented, it's foam that will be flat by lunch.
5. **A three-check criteria bridge** that the brand survives at about 70–75%.
6. **Kill the cheap copy:** "$40 'memory foam' shoes are the same foam that packs flat" (our equivalent of their $59 Amazon shoe), alongside our priced enemies (Hoka, Skechers, Brooks).
7. **Mirror story:** the friend or neighbor who never fixed it (a fall, a cane, stopped traveling) versus mom, who did. Fear without threat.
8. **Social-repair payoff:** she's back at the grandkids' games and the church potluck, laughing.
9. **Stacked P.S. lines:** the test tonight, the warning signs, "check every shoe against the test, *including ours*", the guarantee.
10. **A literal hook-moment image,** including a doorbell or security-camera frame when the story's device is a camera.

**Don't copy:**
- Surgeon or doctor narrators (against our claims rules).
- Bunion "progression stopped" or X-ray claims.
- "Wide toe box" or extra-wide promises.
- BOGO, unless it's in brand.json offers.

**Watch:** whether the surgeon/authority variants (17–34k) fade while the witness stories (7.5–12.5k) keep running. The Monday study re-checks these ids at 14 and 30 days.
