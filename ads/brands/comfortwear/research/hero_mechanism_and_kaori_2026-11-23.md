## 1. What a "hero mechanism" is

### What the board says
From his "hero unique mechanism" diagram, word for word:

```
Angle 1        Angle 2        Angle 3        Angle 4        Angle 5
symptom/problem (×5)
        \         \        |        /        /
              UNIQUE MECHANISM
          "root cause of every problem"
                     |
                   Proof
         "clinical or logical backing"
                     |
                    USP
     "how your product fixes the mechanism"

"Every angle traces back to one root cause. The product fixes that root cause."
"1 mechanism unlocks 10 to 20 angles. Each angle is a new pocket of buyers."
```

### In plain terms
A hero mechanism has three parts:
1. **The root cause** (the unique mechanism of the problem). One specific, nameable reason the buyer
   still has the problem after trying everything else.
2. **The proof.** Clinical evidence, or a chain of logic the buyer can follow in her head: "oh, *this*
   leads to *this*, that's why nothing worked."
3. **The fix** (the unique mechanism of the solution, USP on his board). How *your* product, and not the
   things she already tried, deals with that root cause.

It's the **hero** because you commit everything to it.
- He says the brands that scale "stuck to one thing and scaled the f*** out of that". Smaller brands
  keep testing new mechanisms hoping one will scale.
- You don't need new mechanisms for new buyers. You need **new angles** (symptoms, situations,
  personas, occasions) that all point back to the same one.
- At his example brand (Resilia, a supplement), weight loss, brain fog and energy are all angles on the
  same root cause.
- He warns against "pure Claude slop" mechanisms. Americans have seen so many ads that the logic has to
  hold up.

### Kaori's hero mechanism

How far one mechanism stretches for Kaori:
- **Angles:** husband, grandkids, daughter, widow dating, sister, girls' trip, doctor visit, summer heat.
- **A new product:** a persimmon *shampoo*, sold on the same mechanism ("it lingers in your hair
  longest, the one place soap never reaches").

## 2. "Random symptom stacks → link back to mechanism"

### What his board says (the Resilia board, around 12:50)

> **Angles to push on:** literally anything to do with weight loss · **Random symptom stacks → link back
> to mechanism**, e.g. gut, kidney, parasites · Aging, e.g. fatigue, hair thinning, brain fog
>
> **Mechanisms to push on:** parasites (of course) · stuck poop · gut bacteria imbalance · lymphatic
> drainage (new) · joint pain/inflammation

### What it means
A symptom-stack ad opens with **several symptoms that seem unrelated**: "tired at 3 p.m., bloated after
dinner, hair in the drain, can't lose the last 10 lb". Then it reveals **one hidden cause that connects
them all**, which is the mechanism, which the product fixes.

Why it works:
1. **More ways in.** Each symptom is a self-identification hook. A viewer who doesn't relate to symptom 1
   relates to symptom 3. One ad catches buyers who'd normally need four ads.
2. **The "aha" builds belief.** When unconnected problems suddenly share one cause, the viewer feels she
   discovered the answer, so the mechanism feels true.
3. **It makes the product a single fix for many problems**, which justifies the price and the urgency.
4. **It's still one mechanism**, so it doesn't dilute the hero. It just widens the net.

### Kaori uses a social symptom stack, not a physical one
Kaori's symptoms are moments of social rejection, never body descriptions:
- the husband "stopping in the doorway… not crossing in"
- he stands up from the couch for "another beer" and doesn't sit back down
- the daughter's side hug "with her car keys already in her hand"
- the grandson getting out of the pool: "Not while you are in it"
- the sister's long hug at the door, then whispering to the husband
- grandchildren who "no longer hold on as long"

Each moment is something a 60+ woman has quietly noticed. All of them lead back to nonenal.

### ComfortWear symptom stacks (two versions to test)

Compliance applies to both. Use "this is often your shoes" and "can contribute". No diagnosis, no
treatment claims, no "podiatrist recommended". If a podiatrist appears, they explain; they don't endorse.

**A. Physical stack** (podiatrist or creator voiceover over a body-map graphic):
> "The stab in your heel on the first step out of bed. The ache in your arch by lunch. Knees that hurt
> more than your feet by dinner. Shoes that fit at breakfast and squeeze by 6 p.m. Most women think
> those are four different problems: age, weight, bad knees, swelling. Here's what they often have in
> common. The foam in your shoes collapsed at hour 4, and everything above it has been taking the hit
> since."

**B. Life-moment stack** (Kaori-style, the higher-ceiling one):
> "You started circling the lot for the closest parking spot. You take the elevator now, even for one
> floor. You told the grandkids 'you go ahead, I'll wait here.' You turned down the extra shift you used
> to fight for. Your step counter says 3,000 on days it used to say 10. Nobody says it out loud, but you
> know when your feet started deciding your day. It's usually not your age. It's foam that's been
> collapsing under you by hour 4 every single day."

Stack it with a persona, as always (one of these, not all at once): a nurse version ("you started
sitting at the station between call lights"), a grandma version, or a plus-size version.

### Anatomy of the "Please DON'T buy persimmon soap" ad
This one goes after **people who already bought a cheaper competitor and saw nothing**. It turns their
failure into a reason to buy Kaori.
1. **Contrarian hook:** "Please DON'T buy persimmon soap. I'm begging you… One bar of it cost me my
   doctor of eleven years."
2. **Authority, spoken softly:** her doctor of 11 years says "two words": persimmon soap.
3. **She buys the first cheap one** ($18.99 + $5.99 shipping), does the sniff test every morning, nothing
   changes, and her daughter still hugs her with "keys in hand". She switches doctors in anger.
4. **The reframe (the key move):** the doctor calls her back with six competitor boxes on her desk.
   - *"The brain tunes out a smell that never leaves you."*
   - You can't test it with your own nose, and your daughter won't tell you.
   - "Find a maker who says who it's made for… where to wash with it."
5. **The fix, handed over:** "This isn't a prescription. It's made only for women over sixty. I'm 64,
   and it's what I wash with." "I take the one where somebody else pays if it does nothing for me."
6. **Proof through someone else:** the second Sunday, the daughter hugs with both arms "long enough for
   the porch light to click off".
7. **Close:** "Give it two weeks. Then watch how the people you love say goodbye." Price and guarantee,
   then a P.S.: *"I didn't sit down to sell anybody soap. But he's right, it saves you looking."*

### The "4 types of persimmon soap" video (brand page, 1:57)
> "These two bars both say persimmon soap… there are really four very different things being sold as
> persimmon soap. New brands appear every week, and not all of them are honest…
> 1. **Pure marketing**: an orange bar, a fruity scent… no real persimmon active inside. Persimmon is simply the color.
> 2. **Real persimmon, but only as fruit juice**: very diluted.
> 3. **Concentrated extract**: 'the brands that say 90% persimmon extract'… tells you how much fruit went in, not how much tannin.
> 4. **The approach we take at Kaori**: green, unripe persimmons… we measure the exact tannin content, because tannin is what binds nonenal.
> Ask which kind it is and whether anyone has actually measured the tannin."

This is the classic answer to **market sophistication** (Schwartz stage 3–4): when everyone claims the
same mechanism, *elaborate* it ("measured tannin from unripe fruit") and teach buyers to sort the
category so only you pass.

**Mechanism section ("Why Freshness Changes With Age")**
- "Around midlife, the body begins producing more 2-nonenal. This oil-based compound is linked to the
  persistent 'aging odor' many women notice on their skin, clothes, or bedding."
- The fix: "persimmon extract… rich in natural tannins that help neutralize these odor molecules
  directly at the source instead of simply masking them."
- **No studies are cited.** The page itself carries no "clinically proven" or doctor claims; those live
  in the ad stories.

**Proof**
- "4.7 | 8,000+ Satisfied Customers", with no source or review count shown.
- Three short testimonials, all about **family closeness**: grandkids' hugs, a daughter noticing, a
  granddaughter saying she smells fresh.
- A comparison table: Kaori vs persimmon soap vs normal soap vs body wash.

**Offer**
- $19.95 regular.
- Upsells in the cart: Persimmon Deodorant ($14.99) and Persimmon Shampoo ($34.99).
- **60-day** money-back guarantee. The ads and page also say "eliminate aging odor or your money back".

**FAQ:** sensitive skin, fragrance, where to wash ("behind the ears, around the neck, chest, and across
the back"), how it differs from regular soap, returns.

**The takeaway for structure:** the ad story does all the persuasion. The page only repeats the
mechanism in one line, shows family-closeness proof, and closes. The page's language matches the ads'
emotions exactly ("hug loved ones").

**Science context (ABC News, 2026-11-22; names no brand):**
- The nonenal claim rests on a 2001 study of 22 Japanese people and a 2016 study of 27, and wasn't
  replicated in a 2009 US study.
- The persimmon soap study (2012) had no comparison soap.
- A dermatologist says any soap binds oils.
- A University of Queensland researcher calls out the ads' use of "urgency and shame".

### The playbook in one list
1. **One hero mechanism** (nonenal), named, simple, and logical.
2. **Dozens of angles**, each a social situation for 60+ women (husband, grandkids, daughter, widowhood,
   sisters, friends, doctor, travel, summer).
3. **Long first-person story statics** from persona pages. They read like Facebook posts, not ads.
4. **A mentor or authority character** hands over the product (nurse, doctor), so it's never sold.
5. **Proof through other people's behavior** (hugs, strangers asking), never self-claims.
6. **Defend the category:** "Don't buy…", "4 types", "We should have warned you". Turn competitors'
   disappointed buyers into yours.
7. **Bundle offers and constant urgency.**
8. **Extend the line on the same mechanism** (shampoo).

### What NOT to copy, and why it matters more for ComfortWear
- **Fictional first-person testimonials and a made-up doctor.** The stories ("Becca", "Diane", "Dr.
  Mercer") run from pages named like real women ("Linda Doxey", 41 likes). The FTC's 2024 rule bans
  fake or AI-generated testimonials, and Meta's policies cover misleading identities.
- **"Once a year / tonight only" for 189 days, and "paused production" scarcity.**
- **Weak science.** ABC News is already investigating the category.

ComfortWear already has a trust problem: 1★ reviews about refunds, "drop-shipping scam" and "not
really orthopedic". Copying the deceptive parts would make that worse. **Copy the structure, not the
fiction:**
- Base stories on **real customers**, with permission and with names changed if they want. Or label
  them clearly as dramatizations.
- Use **real experts** only.
- Use **real creators** through Meta partnership ads instead of invented persona pages.
- **Only make offer claims that are true.**

## 4. ComfortWear ads built on the Kaori playbook

### K1. Long-form story static (Kaori's #1 format, which ComfortWear hasn't tested)
- **Stack:** grandma 64 × Disney with grandkids × Foam Collapse.
- **Hook / headline:** *"Last Christmas at Disney my grandson asked his mom why Grandma always waits on the bench. I'm 64. This year I rode Space Mountain."*
- **Beats:**
  - Social symptom stack: waits at the exit, holds the bags, "you go ahead"; the daughter books a
    stroller for *her* "just in case".
  - Mentor: a friend who's a retired nurse (or a real customer) says "it's not your knees, it's your shoes".
  - The press-test moment: her old Skechers flattened.
  - Proof through others: the grandson drags her into line, and the daughter notices at the fireworks.
- **Soft CTA:** "Maybe you don't need new knees. Maybe you need shoes that don't collapse by lunch."
- **Rule:** based on a real customer story, or labeled as a dramatization.

### K2. "Please DON'T buy orthopedic sneakers" (Kaori's "Don't buy" turn-around)
- **Target:** women who already bought cheap "orthopedic" or "comfort" sneakers and were disappointed.
  There are plenty of copycats in this category (orthopedic knit sneakers on Walmart and Amazon).
- **Reframe (your nose-blindness):** *"You can't judge a shoe in the store. Every shoe feels good for 10
  minutes. The test is hour 4."*
- **Sorting rules (true ones only):** does the arch hold when you press it, or is it foam? Is the toe box
  wider than your foot? Does the maker say who it's for (12-hour shifts, standing all day)?
- **Close:** "Give it one full shift. Then notice whether you sit down in your car before you start it."

### K3. "The 4 kinds of 'orthopedic' sneakers" (Kaori's "4 types" video)
1. **Pure marketing:** a regular knit sneaker called "orthopedic".
2. **Memory-foam comfort shoes:** soft at first, flat by lunch.
3. **Max-cushion running shoes:** made for impact, not 12 hours of standing.
4. **A built-in arch and a wide toe box** (ComfortWear).

Only make claims the shoe can show on camera (press test, side-by-side). Check the construction with the
product team first.
