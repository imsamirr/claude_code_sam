### 2. Top hook formulas
1. **Timestamped crisis cold-open** — "It was 3:47 AM…" (#11), "hospital corridor at 2 in the morning" (#8).
2. **Taboo/shame confession** — "I just don't like oral." (#9), "avoid standing next to my thick friends in pictures" (#18).
3. **Misdiagnosis reversal** — "Turns out I don't have plantar fasciitis and you might not either." (#13, #14).
4. **Expert paradox** — "I'm a parasitologist… Then I traveled to Japan." (#6); "Nine out of ten people I see…" (#3).
5. **Unrelated-occupation mystery with a withheld payload** — "I manage an Airbnb… A photo. And five words…" (#15); "I watched a flight attendant hesitate…" (#4).
6. **Credential-of-suffering** — "I beat cancer twice." (#7).
7. **Shock stat** — "7 out of 10 workers…" (#1), "four times more likely to die before they turn 30" (#12).
8. **Spouse-guilt image** — "my husband watched every sunrise alone" (#17).

### 3. Open-loop patterns
- **Master loop planted in line 1–3 and closed in the last 10%** — "five words" (#15), "what I didn't know it was quietly doing to me too" (#12, closed in P.P.S.), barefoot motif (#13), sunrise (#17), "fistfuls of sheets" (#9).
- **"Nobody told me" ladder** — successive "Here's the part I wish someone had told me / Here's something else nobody tells you" (#7).
- **Single-word cliffhangers** — "Fistula." (#10), "Parasites." (#6), "Nuzzle." (#17).
- **"He wasn't finished." / "Then she said something that stopped me cold."** — stacked second reveal inside a scene (#3, #6).
- **Countdown loops** — appointment in N weeks (#11, #12, #14, #19) give the results section built-in suspense.

### 4. Bridge patterns
- **Prescription-then-match**: an expert specifies criteria or doses with no brand, then the narrator finds the one product that matches (#9 Dr. Patel's pad, #14 PT's criteria, #8 buyer's guide, #11 three criteria).
- **Right ingredient, wrong form**: the reader tried the category and it failed; a processing or dose flaw explains why (heat-destroyed anthocyanins #11, filler-padded beetroot #19, heat-dried black walnut #12, "dead foam" #14, jewellery ultrasonic cleaners/fake blue LEDs #8).
- **Elder ritual → "modern version"** (#6 Fumiko, #12 Amish grandmother).
- **Found in the wild**: the product is experienced before it's named (cabin pillow #17, mudroom boots #15, doctor's drawer #13), and the brand appears on a tag, a boarding pass (#4) or a slip of paper (#12).
- **Peer referral** from a support group or forum (#7, #9).
- The product is named at line 1 only in short, product-aware ads (#2, #16).

### 5. Mechanism patterns
- Every story ad has a **2-layer mechanism**: (a) the hidden cause of the problem, and (b) why the usual fixes can't reach it. Then each failed solution is re-explained under the new model ("aimed at the alarm and not at the fraying rope" #13; "medicating the alarm in the hallway while the problem is in the basement" #15).
- **One concrete metaphor does the heavy lifting**: lid/drink (#5), frayed rope (#13), fortress wall biofilm (#6), signal/noise (#18), supply line + idle pump (#15), whipping the brain (#12), walking bone on concrete (#2).
- **Coined or re-named conditions**: "Collapse Fatigue" (#2), "fasciosis" (#13), "ecosystem collapse" (#9), denture stomatitis (#8).
- **Visual proof devices**: dirty water after cleaning (#7 brown, #8 yellowish), lab values to one decimal (#11), a filament test 3/10→8/10 (#15), triple BP readings (#19).

### 6. Length norms
- **Short (2–5k)**: teaser/pre-sell (#1), feature explainer (#2), founder story (#16), status story (#18). These run long (49–105 days) because they're simple and clean to deliver.
- **Mid (5–11k)**: tight stories with one encounter (#3, #4, #5, #7, #8, #9, #17). The strongest longevity cluster (#3 103d, #7 153d, #17 107d).
- **Long (13–28k)**: multi-layer advertorials with failed-competitor sections and P.S. stacks (#6, #10–#15, #19). Mixed results: #11 (213d) and #12 (126d) are long-runners, but #13, #14, #15, #19 ran 0–10 days.

### 7. Long-running vs short-lived
- **Winners (≥100d)**: #11 GoodGrove 213d, #7 Denture Dome 153d, #12 Nutriveen 126d, #17 Nuzzle RV 107d, #18 VitalDrops 105d, #3 Cuurt 103d. What they share: **one vivid, repeatable scene** (bathroom floor, brown water, Target "Okay.", sunrise, group photo, thumb in the shoe heel), **a named and sympathetic second person** (husband, son, Don), and a **clear, visible proof moment**. The two longest also name and fail real competitors with numbers.
- **Short-lived (≤21d)**: #19 (4d) leans hard on anti-doctor, "refuse your medication" framing plus a stitched-on second sales letter; #15 (5d, 28k chars) is the most crafted but very long, with five P.S.s and a narrator-name continuity error ("Jenna" vs "Stephanie"); #13 (0d) has POV errors in its P.S. ("my patients"); #9 (6d) has 21 duplicates, so it was probably scaled across many IDs rather than killed; #4 and #5 (16d) were a paired test. **Lesson:** craft alone doesn't keep an ad alive. Policy-sensitive claims (stopping medication, parasites, children's ADHD, BV) and continuity errors are where short runs cluster.
- **Story beats founder.** For Nuzzle, the customer-story version (#17) was still active at 107 days. The founder version (#16) launched the same day and ended at 85 days.
- **Shared house template.** #9, #11, #12, #14, #15 and #19 reuse identical devices: the doctor saying "Whatever you're doing, keep doing it", "If you're reading this, you see yourself in…", "One path… Another path", "Your timeline may be different. But you won't know until you try", and a fear P.P.S. ("Every day you wait…"). This is one writing playbook spread across many brands. The structure works, but readers may start to recognize the clichés.

### 8. Compliance note
Several high performers (#6, #11, #12, #19, #9, #18) make disease, medication-avoidance or body-change claims that a legitimate brand shouldn't copy. Take the structure (scene, encounter, 2-layer mechanism, form-flaw bridge, measured proof). Don't take the claims.
