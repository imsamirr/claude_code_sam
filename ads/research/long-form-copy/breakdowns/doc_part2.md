## 1. Kitty Supps — Taurine powder for cats (PARTIAL: the top is cut off)

**ID:** kittysupps.com/products/taurine. About 827 words survive. This is the last ~15–20% of a longer ad, most likely the end of Variation 17. Longevity is unknown.

**Narrator / POV:** a cat owner who lost a cat (Simba) and is now saving the two remaining cats (Milo and Star). The credibility comes from a third party: "Dr. Reid", a vet the narrator met at a shelter front desk. Most of the surviving text is vet dialogue.

**Hook:** missing (cut off). The surviving text opens in the middle of the mechanism: *"They replaced traditional grains with cheaper ingredients. Peas. Lentils. Legumes."*

**Section map (surviving tail):**
- 0–30%: vet explains the mechanism with a diagram
- 30–50%: the vet's own failed attempt (raw organs) and the discovery of powder format
- 50–60%: knocking the alternatives ("Chewy and Amazon")
- 60–70%: dosing and safety
- 70–75%: results timeline
- 75–90%: grief and counterfactuals
- 90–100%: restated recommendation, then a fake "EDIT" link drop

**Mechanism:**
- Problem: the "taurine blockade". Legumes block absorption, and *"the high-heat processing that makes kibble destroys most of the taurine."* That leads to no bile, hair that can't move through the gut, and hairballs or a "calcified thing."
- Solution: pure-grade taurine in **powder format** (*"Same principle"* as capsule powder).
- Simplification: *"She pulled out a piece of paper and drew a quick diagram."* This is a napkin-drawing device.

**Product bridge:** the vet herself bridges it: *"Then I found this brand that was creating exactly what I wanted—pure-grade taurine specifically formulated with cats in mind."* The brand is never named in the body. It appears only as a URL in a Reddit-style afterthought: **"EDIT: My bad, I didn't even mention the brand. My DMs are full of questions asking what it is. Here's the link:"** This reads as unsponsored and adds implied social proof ("DMs are full").

**Proof:** "500mg a day", "Within two weeks, the coughing stopped. Within four weeks… hairballs", "three years now", "$6,400 I spent", "$1,400 on diagnostics", "Less than a dollar a day".

**Emotion:** grief and guilt (*"I think about Simba every day"*) turning to a protective warning. The counterfactual anaphora is *"If I hadn't gone to that shelter… If Dr. Reid hadn't been standing… If Milo hadn't coughed…"*

**Objection handling:** overdose fear (*"You can never oversupplement taurine. There's zero toxicity risk."*) and the reframe *"That's not a hairball."*

**Close:** no guarantee in the visible text. There is only the EDIT link.

**Steal this:** let the expert's own failed DIY attempt ("raw organs… too much of a mess") justify the product, then hide the brand until a fake "EDIT: forgot the link" line.

## 2. Alevia — Pomegranate extract (kidney / CKD) — Variation 18

**ID:** alevia.com/pom/17 (an advertorial lander). 5,816 words / about 32.6k characters. This is the longest template in the set. Longevity is unknown.

**Narrator / POV:** furious, grieving widow "Jessica, age 65, Naperville, IL". Credibility comes from lived detail and from the husband's own nephrologist later validating her.

**Hook:** *"My husband died on a dialysis chair earlier this year. He was fine at breakfast on a Tuesday morning. By that afternoon he was gone. He died at a dialysis center in Naperville, Illinois on April 28th at 3:47 PM."*
Type: **death-timestamp confession** (exact place, date and minute).

**Section map:**
- 0–4%: hook and the grief tableau (recliner, pill organizer, EPO syringes)
- 4–6%: **objection mirror**, quoting what readers say in comments (*"My numbers are fine." "I'd know if my kidneys were failing."*)
- 6–7%: reading contract (*"If you're over 55… I know it's long. I know you're scrolling."*)
- 7–14%: backstory. eGFR 47 → 44 → 41 → 39, *"Six quarters of 'monitor and watch.'"*
- 14–17%: specialist verdict (*"we should talk about access placement"*)
- 17–31%: dialysis life montage, with a transplant call that falls through
- 31–37%: death scene (session 642) and the parking lot
- 37–38%: itemized bills
- 38–40%: twist, *her own* eGFR is 58
- 40–44%: research statistics ("Nine in ten people with CKD don't know")
- 44–48%: cabinet "graveyard" of failed products
- 48–52%: Facebook dialysis RN post (the stranger)
- 52–56%: mechanism ("rust")
- 56–62%: own failed attempt with grocery juice
- 62–67%: re-reading the "qualifiers", the five criteria, ancient-culture proof
- 67–70%: brand found, skepticism
- 70–75%: calls the dead husband's doctor, who validates her
- 75–82%: order and results
- 82–88%: four relative/neighbor testimonials
- 88–93%: moral and CTA
- 93–100%: P.S. stack (three of them)

**Open loops:**
- *"I need to tell you what happened to Robert"* → closed by the full arc.
- *"the number on the lab report that changed our lives"* → closed at eGFR 47.
- *"That sentence has lived in my head every day since"* → paid off by the doctor's later contradiction (*"more years"*).
- *"She hadn't just said 'pomegranate'"* → a delayed loop about the RN's qualifiers, closed by the criteria list.
- The pill-organizer motif (Thursday and Friday compartments) is opened in paragraph 3 and closed in the P.S.

**Mechanism:**
- Problem: *"quiet oxidative damage… Like rust building up inside your kidneys"*. Nephrons are non-renewable; *"The nephrons that are gone are gone."*
- Solution: punicalagins (*"Three times the antioxidant power of red wine"*).
- Why other products fail: heat destroys punicalagins, juice carries 32 g sugar, powders are "red dust", Amazon blends are a "proprietary blend".
- Analogy: *"like handing somebody a watering can and telling them they're ready to put out a fire."*

**Product bridge:** the ingredient arrives at about 50% through the RN quote (*"I take a standardized organic pomegranate extract every morning"*), followed by doubt (*"Pomegranate? The fruit?"*). The brand is named only at about 70%: **"I found a company. Alevia. And I was skeptical. God, I was skeptical."** The CTA sells *the article*, not the product: *"The article that explains what I just told you… I want you to read it."*

**Proof:** eGFR series, "Session 642", "$34,000… $11,200… $89,000 a year", "47 minutes lining up the bottles", "5,000 years", "eGFR 57… the slide had stopped", brother-in-law "eGFR came back at 52", a fasting glucose drop from 112 to 104.

**Emotion:** grief, then fury (*"I was shaking. Not from cold. From anger. From grief."*), then cautious hope. The vindication beat is the doctor saying *"He might have had more years before dialysis."*

**Style:** short declaratives, triadic anaphora ("I learned…" ×8, "If anyone had told us. If we'd just known."), Midwestern specifics (Jewel grocery, toaster calendar). Reading level is about grade 5–6.

**Close:** 90-day money-back guarantee tied to "three months" (which mirrors "come back in three months"). The husband's aphorism serves as the CTA: *"The best time to fix the roof is before it rains." It's raining.* The P.S. contrasts $89k a year with *"two capsules a day that cost about two dollars"*. The P.P.S. disclaims affiliation. The P.P.P.S. asks readers to share.

**Steal this:** the "dead spouse → my own matching lab number → the dead spouse's own doctor validates the fix" spine. The guilt-proof frame is "I'm not selling anything; I'm trying not to end up like him."

## 3. Alevia — Amla (cholesterol) — Variation 19

**ID:** alevia.com/amla/33. 3,219 words / 16.8k characters. Longevity is unknown.

**Narrator / POV:** "Cheryl", 57, a billing supervisor from Findlay, Ohio. She is careful with money and not a "Facebook person". Credibility comes from family history (her mother's placemat chart) and from repeatedly saying she kept taking her statin.

**Hook:** *"The Christmas card I mailed to my cousin Patty in November came back to my house in February with a yellow sticker on it that said DECEASED."*
Type: **object-of-death visual hook** plus a meta line (*"if I try to ease into this I'll never write it"*).

**Section map:**
- 0–8%: the card scene (thawing peas against her elbow)
- 8–14%: who Patty was, and the last phone call (*"She said she was fine"*)
- 14–22%: how Patty died (*"She was making coffee"*)
- 22–30%: *"Patty and I were the same person, biologically"*, the family chart, borderline numbers
- 30–42%: doctor visit and a Socratic Q&A that uncovers the mechanism gap
- 42–48%: four nights of reading
- 48–56%: amla, emblicanins, trials, the oxidation loop
- 56–58%: order (brand named)
- 58–72%: honest timeline (weeks 3, 4, 6, 10)
- 72–80%: emotional fallout and the brother
- 80–90%: education recap and reassurance about medication
- 90–100%: guarantee, the card in the drawer, four P.S. lines

**Open loops:**
- *"I want to say something here, because I think it's the heart of why I'm writing this"* → closed by "same person, biologically."
- The brother Mark is introduced at 72% and closed in the P.P.P.S. (*"He has not visited us in 4 years"*).
- The card is closed at the end: *"the next card I send, I want to come to me, signed."*

**Mechanism:**
- Problem: *"LDL by itself isn't really the issue… It's the oxidation that turns it dangerous."* This is revealed through the doctor's own admission: *"The statin… doesn't address oxidation directly."*
- Loop: *"oxidation drives cholesterol production… the whole thing feeds itself. If you only lower the production, you've turned down one side of the loop."*
- Solution: amla emblicanins. 12-week trials, twice daily.

**Product bridge:** the ingredient arrives at about 48% via research. The brand arrives abruptly at 56%: *"I ordered Alevia Amla on a Sunday night."* There is no brand-search beat. The bridge is softened by *"I did not stop my prescription."*

**Proof:** the age and city of every person; the 38-minute call; *"total cholesterol had come down 38 points… LDL 27… HDL ticked up 4 points"*. The doctor read about amla *"in 2 of the medical newsletters"*. The brother's doctor said *"hm."*

**Emotion:** survivor's dread and guilt (*"I had been one good ignored Christmas card away from being Patty"*), relief, family love. The engine is fear of an identical fate rather than anger. It is less furious than Variation 18.

**Style:** numbers written as digits ("2 capsules", "4 nights"), so it reads like a real person's post. Reported dialogue without quote marks (*"He said, well, no."*). Honesty beats (*"The first 3 weeks, I didn't notice anything."*). The light-symptom proof (*"You're laughing more lately"*) is relatable.

**Close:** 90-day guarantee framed as *"the same window the trials I read about had used."* Closing anaphora: *"If you have a chart. If you have a number. If you have a cousin. If you have a feeling. Don't wait until February."* Four P.S. lines: medication disclaimer, guarantee rationale ("billing supervisor for 22 years"), the brother's visit, and *"I'm guessing you have a Patty in your life."*

**Steal this:** make the mechanism come out of the doctor's own mouth through a naive "what does the number actually mean?" question. The doctor concedes the gap, and you never have to attack medicine.

**Close:** 30-day money-back guarantee. A self-tracking instruction (*"Track your movements… bring the results to your doctor"*). The P.S. gives a timeline recap and the P.P.S. a story recap.

**Steal this:** reframe the symptom as the *last* stage of a hidden process (*"That's the end of the process. Not the beginning."*), so the standard drug is treating the tail.

- 60–64%: *"I'm not… against inhalers"*
- 64–66%: VelvaFlow found
- 66–80%: day 9, week 2, week 4, week 8 results, then two patient outcomes
- 80–95%: two choices, check-engine-light metaphor, *"I wish someone had told me"*

**Close:** 60-day money-back guarantee (*"Use the whole bottle. If your oxygen still crashes when you stand up, send it back."*). The sign-off repeats the credentials. The P.P.S. **pre-frames a scary side effect as proof**: *"The dark stuff… will look alarming… The darker it is, the longer it was stuck."*

**Steal this:** give readers a home self-test (sit, stand, watch the number) that turns their own body into the hook, and pre-frame any visible "purge" as evidence that it is working.

## C. Open-loop patterns
- **Object motifs** opened in the first 5% and closed in the P.S.: pill organizer, pharmacy bag, Christmas card, the pebble, the dress, the recycling bin. These are the strongest emotional bookends in the set.
- **The doctor's fatal sentence** ("The nephrons that are gone are gone") reversed later by the same doctor ("He might have had more years").
- **The delayed re-read:** the stranger's quote is first misread, then re-read for its "qualifiers". This opens a loop at about 50% that closes the criteria/spec section at about 65%.
- **The pre-empted objection loop:** "And the beet powder. I'll get to the beet powder." (V30)
- **The withheld name:** "The thing I'm not supposed to write" (Fora, 87%); "EDIT: I didn't even mention the brand" (Kitty).
- **Narrator-named loops** in the literary piece ("this is the bridge"; "Hold that thought").
- Loops are usually **stacked three or four deep** by about 40%: the relative's death, the reader's own number, the stranger's tip and the qualifier. Each closes in reverse order.

## D. Bridge patterns
- **Ingredient first, brand second, 10–30 points apart.** The ingredient lands at 42–57% and the brand at 56–87%. Typical pairs: pomegranate 42% → Alevia 70%; amla 48% → 56%; elderberry 52% → Elvera 64%; botanicals 55% → VelvaFlow 64%; tart cherry 57% → Ravea 74%; nitrate 48% → BeetWise 71%; stick 63% → Fora 87%.
- **The brand arrives as a search result after failed versions:** "After the third brand failed, I found Elvera"; "I found a company. Alevia. And I was skeptical."; "almost every product… fails at least one of those criteria."
- **The brand arrives through a trusted third party:** a forum review (Mama Bear), a sister (Vylaras), a vet (Kitty), a pharmacist's sister (Fora).
- **Exceptions:** Vylaras (27%), an experience product where the payoff scenes are the proof. GLOV (58–75%) is short copy with a flat "that is genuinely the whole change" reveal.
- The brand line is usually **short and isolated**: "I found a company. Alevia." / "Ravea." / "GLOV GLP-1 Cutting Mix."

## E. Mechanism patterns
1. **"Manages the number, not the cause."** This appears in almost every health ad. The standard drug is conceded to "do its job" but targets the surface: MiraLAX treats output but not the bacteria; inhalers widen the doorway but leave the layer; statins cut production but not oxidation; gabapentin masks pain but leaves the nerves starving; NSAIDs silence the alarm while the enzymes keep eating; lisinopril holds the number while the wall stiffens; cryo removes the growth but not the keratin. The product is positioned as **"alongside, not instead of."**
2. **The symptom is the last stage** of a hidden, decade-long process (bacterial collapse, accumulation, oxidative rust).
3. **Why the cheap version fails** is almost always one of five things: *heat destroys it* (punicalagins, nitrate, anthocyanins ×2, taurine), *wrong route* (oral magnesium, swallowed herbs "<1%"), *underdosed* (2–4% vs 10%; 5–10 mg vs 300–500 mg), *wrong contact time* ("can't dissolve a callus in ninety seconds"), or *sugar/fillers/proprietary blend*.
4. **Criteria checklist:** organic, standardized extract, no heat, third-party tested, small batch. The product is then the only one passing all five.
5. **Simplifiers:** rust in the kidney's filters, a storm door spring, scale in an old pipe, a callus, repainting a house with a crumbling foundation, a check-engine light covered with tape, a watering can at a fire, "Same fire. Different room.", a napkin diagram.
6. **Ancient or cross-cultural proof:** Central Asia "fruit of life" for 5,000 years; Eastern European "blood food"; Sardinian grandmothers for a thousand years plus TCM, Ayurveda, Amish and Persian traditions ("when independent traditions arrive at the same conclusion…").

## F. Proof and close conventions
- **The doctor-validation line** at the follow-up: "Whatever you're doing, keep doing it" (V18, V30), "Whatever you're doing is working" (V19), "Continue what you're doing" (V20), "Your sensation is better?" (V21b).
- **Week-stamped timelines** with one small, human, non-clinical win before the lab win: walking the dog at 6:15, "You're laughing more lately", walking past the cupboard, the husband zipping a dress.
- **Honest ceilings** that raise credibility: "I'm not telling you my kidneys healed"; "136 over 91 isn't normal"; "I'm not going to tell you my legs are 30 again."
- **Guarantees** of 30, 60 or 90 days, framed in story terms ("the same window the trials used"; "Use the whole bottle").
- **Scarcity:** "small company… sells out" (Fora, Ravea); "big tech buying it" (Vylaras); only Mama Bear says "not in stores".
- **P.S. stacks** of one to four lines: a cost contrast ($89k a year vs $2 a day), a disclaimer that the narrator isn't affiliated, side-effect pre-framing, an extra testimonial, "share with anyone over 55".
- **The CTA often sells an article or click, not a purchase:** "read it… for whoever you'd leave behind"; "The article below… Twenty minutes."

## G. Length norms
- Flagship templates run 5.8k–6.4k words (32–35k characters): Alevia V18 and BeetWise V30.
- Specialist and relative templates run 2.4k–3.6k words (13–20k characters).
- Native testimonials run 430–1,000 words (2.4–5.3k characters).

Long ads are not padded. The length comes from scene montage (dialysis or rehab life) and testimonial chains, both of which are emotional proof.

## H. Longevity
The doc has **no run-time data**, so we cannot compare winners with short-lived ads. One proxy signal: the Alevia V18 skeleton was **cloned by another advertiser** (BeetWise, V30), and its stock lines appear in Fora. Copywriters only clone what scales, so V18's structure is the strongest implied winner here. The clone also shows how iterations get safer: more "keep taking your medication", more modest results, and third-party test numbers.

## I. QA warnings for anyone cloning
Template swaps leave continuity errors:
- Elvera: brother at 58 vs father at 63.
- Ravea: the story is about a spoonful of concentrate but the product is capsules.
- Elvera: a stray "beetroot capsule" analogy left over from a beet ad.

Real-brand name-drops create compliance risk: Hyatt, Marriott and Saatva (Hanare); NATO (Vylaras). Rosabella is the only ad with full compliance hygiene (an asterisked claim, an FDA disclaimer, a dated Amazon rating, and "nobody here is going to promise you a transformation").
